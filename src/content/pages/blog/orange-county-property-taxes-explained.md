Two questions about Orange County property tax reach me more than any other. A bill comes in higher than the lender estimated. Or a supplemental bill arrives and the owner is certain the county made a mistake.

Usually it did not. The county is collecting the difference between the old assessed value and yours, for the months you have owned the house.

Read the bill for that parcel. That is the method, and it beats any countywide average you will find online.

## How the Orange County property tax bill is built

The 1% in Proposition 13 is the base levy, not the total. The bill has four layers, and only the first two follow your assessed value.

| Layer | What it pays for | Based on assessed value? | Who sets it |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1% general levy | County, city, school, and district operations | Yes | Fixed by the California Constitution |
| Voter-approved bonded debt | School, community college, water, and other district bonds | Yes, as a rate per $100 of assessed value | Voters in each district |
| Special assessments and Mello-Roos | Services and Community Facilities District bond repayment | Usually no | The levying district |
| Direct charges | Sewer, vector control, lighting, other district charges | Usually no | The levying district |

Every parcel sits in a tax rate area: a geographic area, often just a few blocks, where the same combination of districts applies. Your rate is the sum of what applies there. The Auditor-Controller publishes the [tax rate book](https://ocauditor.gov/about/central-accounting/property-tax-accounting/tax-rate-book/) each year, and the county's [property tax guide for new homeowners](https://www.octreasurer.gov/sites/ttc/files/import/data/files/118057.pdf) shows the same formula on page 4.

Two houses on one street can sit in different rate areas if a district line runs between them. I will not give you "the Orange County rate." It would be wrong for the house you buy, and a calculator that has never seen your parcel can't do better.

## Proposition 13 in plain English

Three ideas explain most of the bill.

**Base year value.** Buy a house and the assessor generally sets the assessed value at the price you paid. That is the base. New construction gets one when completed.

**The 2% factor.** The base is adjusted for inflation each year, by no more than 2%. The result is the factored base year value, and that is what you are taxed on. The Board of Equalization [explains the mechanics](https://boe.ca.gov/proptaxes/pdf/pub29.pdf).

**Reassessment on sale.** A change of ownership resets the base to market value. The cap applies between sales, not across them.

A neighbor who bought thirty years ago may be taxed on a base far below today's value. You pay on the price you just agreed to. Same house, different bill. That is how an acquisition value system works, not a loophole. Long-held blocks, the kind common in parts of [Anaheim](/anaheim-ca-home-for-sale/), often show a wide gap between taxed value and market price.

Selling and buying at once? The reset generally follows you to the next house. The [seller's guide](/sellers/) covers the timing side.

## Mello-Roos, without the mythology

A Mello-Roos district is a Community Facilities District, created under the Community Facilities Act of 1982. Local governments use it to fund roads, sewers, parks, schools, and fire stations in new growth areas. The district sells bonds and repays them with a special tax on the properties inside it.

**It is not based on your home's value.** It is a special tax, apportioned by a formula the district adopted. That formula can use square footage, lot size, land use, or a flat charge per parcel. Very different houses can owe the same amount. On the bill it prints in the special assessment charges section, next to a district name and a phone number.

**It does not follow the 2% cap.** Proposition 13's inflation factor applies to assessed value, not special taxes. The district's own rate schedule governs increases, within the maximum set when the district formed. If someone says Mello-Roos is capped at 2%, they are describing Prop 13.

**It usually ends with the bonds, but not always.** Most districts levy until the bonds are repaid. Some keep a smaller levy for maintenance, and a district that still has bonds left to issue can keep collecting past the current maturity date. I wouldn't promise a buyer that any line disappears in a given year. Ask the district how long the levy runs and what it is scheduled to do.

Four ways to check a home:

1. Pull the parcel's tax bill. The charge appears under special assessment charges with a district name and phone number.
2. Look up the address on the county's [Mello-Roos map](https://mello.ocgov.com/). It shows the base levy and the special assessments for that parcel.
3. Ask for the notice of special tax. Sellers in a district have a disclosure duty, and it states the current levy and the maximum.
4. Read the preliminary title report.

It also changes what you can borrow. Lenders generally include the special tax in the housing payment for debt-to-income, so $3,000 a year is $250 a month. Use the [mortgage page](/mortgage/) with the full tax line.

Irvine is where buyers meet this often, because so much of the city came through planned communities. The [Irvine page](/irvine-ca-home-for-sale/) covers that market.

## The supplemental bill

You close in October. The annual bill mailed that fall is still in the seller's name, based on the seller's assessed value. Escrow prorates it between you and the seller for the days each of you owned the house. That part is handled.

Then the assessor values the property at your price and creates a supplemental assessment for the difference. The county mails supplemental bills only a few times a year, usually three to six months after the purchase, so yours may land in winter. It covers the transfer through June 30, prorated by month. The [Treasurer-Tax Collector's FAQ](https://octreasurer.gov/faq/supplemental-property-taxes) walks through the timing.

Close from June through December and expect one bill. Close from January through May and expect two: the rest of this tax year, and the full following year. The Auditor-Controller publishes the [proration factor for each purchase month](https://ocauditor.gov/about/central-accounting/property-tax-accounting/supplemental-prorations/).

The amount catches people because it is not in the impound account. Your lender funded that account for the annual bill at the old value. Supplemental taxes are generally not covered, and escrow does not pay them.

### A worked example, with assumptions

Close on October 15 at $900,000. The seller's assessed value was $520,000. The difference is $380,000.

Assume a 1.1% combined rate. That is an assumption, not a quote. Your bill states the real one.

$380,000 × 1.1% = $4,180 a year.

The county prorates by whole months to June 30, and the Auditor-Controller's chart gives an October purchase a factor of 0.67, which is eight of the twelve. $4,180 × 0.67 = $2,800. Eight twelfths of $4,180 is $2,787. Plan on roughly $2,800, and treat the rate on your own bill as the only real input.

Close in March instead and the same gap is billed twice: 0.25 for the rest of this fiscal year and 1.00 for the next. On the same numbers that is $5,225, not $2,800.

If you paid less than the assessed value, the supplemental assessment is negative and you may get a refund. The annual bill still stands.

If the assessment itself looks wrong, you have 60 days from the mailing date to file an assessment appeal with the Clerk of the Board. The [Assessor's page on supplemental assessments](https://www.ocassessor.gov/page/supplemental-assessments-and-notices) covers the appeal window and the payment schedule.

## The Homeowners' Exemption

The easiest money on the page.

It removes $7,000 from your assessed value if the home is your principal residence and you owned and occupied it on January 1. The Assessor puts the saving at $70 a year at the 1% general rate and calls that a minimum, because the bond rates ride on assessed value too. The [Assessor's page](https://www.ocassessor.gov/tax-saving-programs/homeowners-exemptions) has the form and the rules.

File by February 15 for the full amount. A claim filed from February 16 through December 10 generally receives 80%, or $5,600 of assessed value. Form BOE-266. New owners usually get a pre-filled application within 90 days of the deed recording. If it hasn't arrived by then, call 714-834-3821. You file once, and it renews while you own and occupy the home.

Two housekeeping notes. If you move out and rent the house, file the termination notice by December 10 to end the exemption without a penalty. And if you sell, tell the assessor, because the exemption does not travel with you.

## What to check before you make an offer

Five checks, all cheaper than finding out after your contingency period.

**The current tax bill.** Search the parcel at taxbill.octreasurer.gov or ask me. Read the total, then the special assessment lines. That is close to what you will pay after the supplemental period.

**The CFD notice.** Ask for the notice of special tax. It states the current levy, the maximum, and the bond maturity. If nobody can produce it, that tells you something.

**Supplemental exposure.** Price minus current assessed value, times the rate on the bill, prorated to June 30. If the gap is wide, plan for a real one-time payment.

**The assessment basis.** If the current value sits near market, your supplemental bill will be small. If it sits far below, it will not be.

**The monthly total.** Principal, interest, insurance, HOA, and the full tax line. A cheaper house with a district charge can cost more per month than a pricier one without.

That is why I ask for the tax bill before we write an offer. Walking away then is free. Walking away after the appraisal is not.

## Inherited property and Prop 19

Since February 16, 2021, a child or grandchild who inherits a family home can keep the parent's or grandparent's base year value only if the home becomes their principal residence. The market value also has to stay within the transferred value plus $1,000,000, adjusted every two years for inflation. Above that, the excess is added to taxable value. The Board of Equalization publishes the [current rules](https://www.boe.ca.gov/prop19/).

Two deadlines: the homeowners' exemption claim within one year of the transfer, and the exclusion claim within three years or before a sale to a third party. Miss them and the exclusion can be lost.

Proposition 19 also lets owners 55 and older, and severely disabled owners, move their base year value to a replacement home anywhere in California, up to three times. Victims of wildfire or a governor-declared natural disaster can transfer a base year value to a replacement home too. If a parent's house is part of your plans, talk to the assessor and a CPA before escrow.
