Most of the worry I hear is not about a defect. It is about the sentence that has to go next to it.

A seller tells me the hall bathroom was remodeled before he bought the place and there is no permit for it. Should he write that down? Yes. Then comes the real question, which is whether writing it down kills the sale.

Sometimes it costs a small credit. Staying quiet can cost far more, and the bill arrives a year after closing with a contractor's letter attached.

Here is how the disclosure side of a California sale works. The wider sequence is in my [seller's guide](/sellers/). I am an agent, not an attorney, so read this as a map of the forms and the deadlines, not as legal advice.

## The forms you will sign

Four documents do most of the work. They arrive together and they overlap on purpose.

| Form | Who produces it | Authority | When |
|---|---|---|---|
| Transfer Disclosure Statement (TDS) | You, plus each agent's inspection section | Civil Code 1102 et seq., form at 1102.6 | Before acceptance, or as soon as practicable before closing |
| Agent Visual Inspection Disclosure (AVID) | Both agents, separately | Civil Code 2079 | With the TDS |
| Natural Hazard Disclosure (NHD) | A third-party company, ordered by escrow or your agent | Civil Code 1103 | Early escrow |
| Seller Property Questionnaire (SPQ) | You | C.A.R. form, required by the purchase agreement | With the TDS |

**The TDS is the statutory one.** A blunt checklist. Appliances and systems first, then roof, foundation, plumbing, electrical, water intrusion, unpermitted work, code violations, neighborhood noise, lawsuits. You answer from what you know.

**The SPQ is longer.** Repairs, insurance claims, disputes, deaths in the last three years, anything else a buyer would want. Not a statute, but the purchase agreement requires it, so treat it the same.

**The NHD is not yours to write.** The duty is yours, or your agent's. A company runs your address against the state and federal hazard maps and sells you the report: flood, dam inundation, very high fire severity, wildland fire, earthquake fault, seismic hazard. Section 1103(d) makes any waiver of that article void as against public policy, and section 1102(c) says the same about the TDS.

**Three more.** Pre-1978 homes need the [federal lead paint disclosure](https://www.epa.gov/lead/real-estate-disclosures-about-potential-lead-hazards), the EPA pamphlet, and any records you have, with ten days for the buyer to test unless the parties agree in writing to a different period or the buyer waives it. A condo or planned development needs the association's documents and budget under Civil Code 4525 and following. Every contract for a one to four unit home also carries the Megan's Law notice pointing buyers to the state's registered sex offender website (Civil Code 2079.10a).

If you want the state's version of all this, the Department of Real Estate publishes a free booklet, [Disclosures in Real Property Transactions](https://www.dre.ca.gov/files/pdf/re6.pdf).

## What changed in 2026: three new disclosure sections

They took effect on January 1, 2026, and they appear in the package you will sign this year.

- **Electrical.** Section 1102.6i requires a written advisory telling the buyer it may be advisable to have the electrical system inspected, including the main service panel, subpanels, and wiring. It does not apply to a building within three years of its certificate of occupancy.
- **Gas appliances.** Section 1102.6j requires you to disclose any state or local rule about replacing the gas appliances staying with the house, if you or your agent know about it.
- **Smoking residue.** Section 1102.6k, from AB 455, puts the duty on you alone to disclose in writing any known tobacco or nicotine residue, or a history of occupants smoking or vaping on the property. Actual knowledge, not an investigation.

If your agent hands you a form you have not seen before, that is why. Read the current version, not the copy in a 2021 file.

## The timing rule, and the buyer's three-day right to cancel

Civil Code section 1102.3(a) sets the deadline in one sentence. The Transfer Disclosure Statement goes to the buyer as soon as practicable before transfer of title.

That is a soft rule with a hard consequence. If a required disclosure arrives after the purchase agreement is signed, the buyer gets a window to walk. Three days after personal delivery. Five after mail, or five after electronic delivery where the parties agreed to transact that way. Written notice to you or your agent.

Most packages I send go out with the offer, before acceptance. Then the statutory window is not the buyer's escape hatch. Their investigation contingency is. The [escrow timeline](/blog/california-escrow-process-orange-county/) is where that plays out, and the standard purchase agreement adds its own clock: seven days after acceptance for the seller to deliver the package, seventeen for the buyer to investigate.

One detail gets missed. The clock starts only when the seller's sections are complete and delivered, plus the agent's section if you are represented. A half-filled TDS handed over to look responsive does not start it.

The same rule covers corrections. Under section 1102.9 an amendment follows section 1102.3, so telling the buyer in week three that the roof leaked in February restarts their three or five days. That is not a reason to sit on a correction. It is a reason to get the first version right.

None of this is free to ignore. Section 1102.13 makes a seller who willfully or negligently fails to comply liable for the buyer's actual damages.

## What counts as a material fact

A material fact affects the value or desirability of the property, is known to you, and is not something a diligent buyer would find on their own.

The form asks about a lot of it. Not all of it. Civil Code section 1102.8 says listing items for disclosure does not limit any other duty that exists under another law or to avoid fraud or deceit.

Past the checkboxes, this is the list I walk sellers through:

- **Water.** Where it came in, when, what you did, whether it came back. A patched ceiling stain is a fact even if the roof is fine now.
- **Unpermitted work.** Any addition, conversion, patio cover, or panel swap without a final inspection.
- **Insurance.** A denied claim, a non-renewal, a carrier that refused the house over the roof or the panel. The next carrier will ask.
- **Neighbors and boundaries.** A fence line that misses the survey, an easement someone uses, a shared driveway dispute.
- **Code enforcement and litigation.** Open permits, citations, a notice to abate, anything filed that touches the property.
- **HOA.** A pending special assessment, litigation against the board, a rental cap, roofs the reserve study says are due.
- **Environmental.** Asbestos in old flooring, a buried oil tank, a former use that left contamination.

Deaths are separate. Civil Code section 1710.2 says a death more than three years before the buyer's offer is not a material fact you must disclose. The same section does not immunize an intentional misrepresentation about a death if the buyer asks. So the safe answer is the honest one.

An insurance claim you were paid for still belongs in the disclosure. The repair is the good news. The claim is the record.

## As-is does not mean silence

An as-is clause is a repair allocation. The buyer takes the property in its present condition and cannot demand fixes. That is real.

It says nothing about what you knew.

California courts have drawn that line for sixty years. Lingsch v. Savage (1963) 213 Cal.App.2d 729, 742, is still cited for it. An as-is provision means the buyer takes the property in the condition visible or observable to them, and it does not shield a seller who fails to disclose a condition the buyer cannot see. The court's reasoning was blunt: reading the clause any wider would let a seller contract around his own fraud, which California law does not allow.

An inspection does not change that. An inspector reports what is visible and accessible that day, not what you knew and did not write down.

So the two sentences that do not belong in one conversation are "we are selling as-is" and "so I do not have to mention it."

Write it down, then sell as-is. The clause still does its job on repairs. The disclosure does its job on the truth. What an inspector will and will not catch is its own topic, and I wrote about the [limits of a California home inspection](/blog/home-inspection-california-what-buyers-should-know/) separately.

## Unpermitted work

This comes up in every older Orange County house I list. A converted garage in Garden Grove. A bonus room in Anaheim. A patio enclosure in Westminster. A panel swap nobody pulled a permit for.

Start with the record. Pull the permit history from the city, then compare permitted square footage to what the assessor has and what is standing on the slab. Most north Orange County cities let you do this online in an afternoon.

If you are accepting an offer within 18 months of the day you took title, section 1102.6h adds another list: the additions, alterations, and repairs you contracted for since then, plus the contractor's name and contact information. That is the flipper rule, and it is easy to miss on a quick resale.

Then you get one of three answers.

**It was permitted.** Disclose it anyway, with the number. It answers a question the inspector will raise.

**Never permitted, looks compliant.** Say when it was done, by whom if you know, and that no permit appears in the record. Buyers accept this when the work is old and clearly done well.

**Never permitted, visibly not to code.** Disclose, get a contractor's bid to legalize or remove it, and expect that number in negotiation. The credit is usually smaller than the reduction a buyer takes in their own head.

Legalizing after the fact is slow, some cities require opening walls, and it can surface other problems. With three weeks before listing, that is not a plan. Price it and disclose it.

Never write "square footage is permitted" when you do not know. That is a representation. "Buyer to verify" is not a disclosure either. It is a hope, and it does not survive a lawyer. What a credit does to your net is in the [cost to sell in Orange County](/blog/cost-to-sell-a-house-orange-county/) breakdown.

## What you are not required to disclose

This section is shorter than sellers want, and it should be read with an attorney.

**A death more than three years before the offer.** Section 1710.2(a)(1)(A) removes it from the list. Read that as a shield against liability, not a license to mislead. Subdivision (d) says it does not protect an intentional misrepresentation about a death in response to a direct question.

**An occupant's HIV status.** Section 1710.2(a)(1)(B) says it is not a material fact. Subdivision (c) is careful to say nothing there relieves anyone of the duty to disclose the physical condition of the premises.

**Protected characteristics.** You do not describe a neighborhood by who lives in it. Fair housing law governs that, and it is not a close call.

**Your opinions.** "I think the furnace has a few years left" is not required. Its age is, and so is the 2022 invoice.

Notice what is absent. There is no exemption for "I never asked," none for "my agent handled it," and none for a buyer who waived inspections.

## When an exemption might apply

Civil Code section 1102.2 exempts a set of transactions. The categories are narrow and specific. New homes sold under a public report. Court-ordered sales, including probate. Foreclosure sales. Fiduciary transfers in the administration of a trust, guardianship, conservatorship, or decedent's estate. Transfers between co-owners, to a spouse, or to a blood relative. Transfers to or from a government entity.

Do not assume your sale fits because it feels unusual. Read the section, or have someone read it for you.

Here is the carve-out that surprises families. Section 1102.2(d) exempts fiduciary transfers, then takes part of it back. The exemption does not apply if the trustee is a natural person, is a trustee of a revocable trust, and was a former owner of the property or occupied it within the preceding year. Note where the one-year limit sits: it applies to occupancy, not to prior ownership. That is the family trust selling mom's house, where the daughter who is trustee lived there while she cared for her.

An exemption from the article is not an exemption from the common law. The duty to disclose known facts a buyer cannot discover exists outside the statute. For a probate or trust sale, that is a fifteen minute conversation with a real estate attorney, and it is worth the fee. The authority question is covered in [selling an inherited house in Orange County](/blog/selling-inherited-house-orange-county/).

## How to fill the forms without hurting yourself

Six rules. They have not changed in twenty years.

**Facts, not conclusions.** "Stain on the ceiling in the rear bedroom, 2023, patched by a roofing contractor, no recurrence" is a fact. "Roof in good condition" is a conclusion you will own if it is wrong.

**Do not guess.** Where the form allows unknown, use it and say why. Answering no to a question you never investigated is worse than answering unknown.

**Date and initial every change.** No correction fluid, no quietly reprinted page. If a page is replaced, say so. Then keep the paper: invoices, permit numbers, inspection reports, claim numbers, the letter from the city.

**Supplement immediately.** An amendment restarts the buyer's window, which is a feature of the rule. It proves you corrected the record when you knew.

**Answer the question asked.** The SPQ asks about neighborhood conditions. Describe the train line, the freeway noise, the kitchen exhaust, the school traffic. Do not describe the people.

**Read the whole package.** The agents fill in their own sections, and you should still read what they wrote. What the buyer was told is the document a dispute gets built on.

The one I repeat most: disclosures do not lose sales. Surprises do. A buyer who reads about the 2019 slab repair on day three prices it and moves on. A buyer who finds it in a contractor's report on day seventeen feels misled, and that feeling costs more than the repair.

## The short version

You owe the buyer a full set of California seller disclosures: the TDS, the NHD, the questionnaire your contract requires, and a written account of what you know, before title transfers. Deliver late and the buyer gets three or five days to change their mind. As-is changes who pays for repairs, not who tells the truth.
