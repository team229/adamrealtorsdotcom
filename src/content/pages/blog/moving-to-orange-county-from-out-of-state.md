Most calls I take from out of state begin with a listing. Someone has an offer in Irvine, or they are moving back to be near parents, and three links arrive before we have talked about anything else.

The problems that actually cost out of state buyers money are almost never about the house. They are about the rules around it.

I have taken these calls from Texas, Arizona, the Midwest and the East Coast. The questions repeat. So do the surprises.

**Your property tax starts at the price you pay.** Not what the last owner paid.

**California income tax starts when you become a resident.** Not when the old house sells.

**The insurance quote can be harder than the mortgage.** Especially near the hills.

**The school follows the address.** Not the city name in the listing.

**The house will not look like the house you left.** Different lot, age and construction.

## The money is different here

Property tax first, because it is the number people misjudge.

Under Proposition 13, assessed value resets to your purchase price when you buy, then can rise no more than 2% a year while you own it, per the [State Board of Equalization](https://www.boe.ca.gov/proptaxes/decline-in-value). The base levy is 1%, and voter approved bonds and parcel charges sit on top of that.

Do the arithmetic before you fall for a house. A household paying $6,000 a year in their old state, buying at $1,100,000 here, is looking at $11,000 at the 1% base alone. The full mechanics are in [the property tax guide](/blog/orange-county-property-taxes-explained/). Pull the actual bill for any address you are serious about.

State income tax is the second line. California taxes residents on all income. In the year you arrive you are likely a part year resident, with a different return and different rules. I send every client to a CPA on this. It is not a question an agent should answer.

Insurance is third. California has been a difficult market to place, and some addresses near the hills and canyons need a separate fire policy. When no standard carrier will write the property, the [California FAIR Plan](https://www.cfpnet.com/) provides basic fire coverage as an insurer of last resort, not a full homeowners policy. Most people pair it with a difference in conditions policy to fill the gaps, and the pair can cost more than a standard policy would.

Get the quote on the exact address before your inspection contingency comes off. A quote that lands late can change the payment.

The fourth line is HOA dues. Orange County has a lot of associations, and they are not only condos. Many single family tracts carry one. Dues are set by a board, can rise, and special assessments happen. Read the budget, the reserve study, the recent minutes, and the rental cap if you plan to rent the house out later.

## The housing stock will not look like where you came from

Lots are smaller than most Midwest and Texas buyers expect. A 6,000 square foot lot is normal in the flat parts of the county, and some are smaller. If you are coming from a quarter acre with a fenced back yard, the first month of showings will feel tight.

The flat parts of Anaheim, Orange, Fullerton and Garden Grove were largely built in the postwar decades. That means single story ranches, three bedroom two bath plans, and closets sized for 1962. The hills and the newer master planned areas have larger homes on steeper ground.

Construction is stucco over wood frame on a concrete slab, with tile roofs common. Few houses here have basements, which surprises people from the East and Midwest more than anything else in this post. Whatever lived in your basement needs a new home: the garage, a shed, or a paid storage unit.

Older homes often have a two car garage and a driveway that fits two more. In denser areas street parking is tight and some cities require permits. Many cities restrict RV and boat parking, so the trailer that lived in your side yard may not come with you. Accessory dwelling units are the newer wrinkle. State law requires cities to allow them, but size, parking and setback rules vary by city, and a next door ADU changes parking and the feel of a street. Ask the city what is permitted at that address.

## Neighborhoods are not cities

City names are shorthand here, and they work less well than almost anywhere else.

Anaheim is the clearest example. The city runs from the flat streets near Disneyland out to Anaheim Hills, the hilly eastern portion of the same city. Same city, different feel, and often a different insurance picture. The [Anaheim page](/anaheim-ca-home-for-sale/) covers the flat side. I go through the hills in [Moving to Anaheim Hills](/blog/moving-to-anaheim-hills/), so I will not repeat it here.

Irvine is another. It is not one place. It is a set of villages, each built around its own shopping center and school assignment, and the village name tells you more about daily life than the city name does. Two Irvine addresses ten minutes apart can sit in different attendance areas. The [Irvine page](/irvine-ca-home-for-sale/) breaks the villages down.

Then the coast. Newport Beach and Costa Mesa share a border that runs down a street in places, and the price per square foot changes as you cross it. The [Newport Beach page](/newport-beach-ca-home-for-sale/) covers the coastal side.

The practical fix is to stop shopping cities and start shopping addresses. Pick two or three, then compare the specific street, lot and school. Then confirm the school assignment with the district before you fall for the house.

## Schools are assigned by address

Families buy for a school and learn after closing that the boundary runs down the middle of the street.

District boundaries do not follow city limits. They follow their own lines, drawn decades ago and adjusted since. The county is split among many districts, not one, and they touch each other in ways that are not obvious from a map with city names on it.

The method that works is boring and reliable. Take the exact street address. Enter it in the district's own school locator. Then call the district and ask for confirmation in writing. Ask one more question while you are on the phone: has the attendance boundary for this address changed in the last few years? Districts redraw lines when enrollment shifts.

During a search, a rental address can matter. If you are leasing while you look, a lease is commonly accepted as proof of residence, so your kids can start school while you keep looking. Ask the district what it accepts before you sign, since the answer varies.

One caution on listings. Portal sites and agent remarks sometimes carry a school name that was accurate years ago. Treat any school name in a listing as a lead to verify, not a fact.

## Moving to Orange County: the first month list

The first month has deadlines, and two are short.

The DMV requires a new resident to register an out of state vehicle within 20 days of becoming a resident or bringing the vehicle into California, per the [DMV's New to California page](https://www.dmv.ca.gov/portal/driver-education-and-safety/special-interest-driver-guides/new-to-california/). Taking a California job starts that clock too. A California driver's license is due within 10 days of becoming a resident, per the DMV's [Fast Facts on license requirements](https://www.dmv.ca.gov/portal/file/fast-facts-5-requirements-for-a-california-drivers-license/).

The license clock starts on your residency date, not the day the truck arrives. The vehicle clock starts at residency, at a California job, or when the car enters the state, whichever comes first. Residency is a facts and circumstances test, and the DMV lists the indicators on that page. Register to vote, enroll a child in school or take a California job, and you are pointing at residency.

| Task | Timing | Where |
|---|---|---|
| Register the vehicle | 20 days after residency or a California job | DMV |
| Smog certification | Before registration for most gasoline vehicles | Licensed station |
| California driver's license | 10 days after residency | DMV, appointment |
| Utilities in your name | Before move in | City utility or the local district |
| Insurance requote | Before contingencies come off | Your carrier or broker |
| Voter registration | Before the next election deadline | County Registrar |
| Pet license | When you arrive | OC Animal Care or your city |
| Bank, mail forwarding, address changes | First two weeks | Bank, USPS, employers |

The smog check catches people. Orange County is on the DMV's list of counties that require smog certification, and the DMV's out of state registration guide requires a California smog certificate for gasoline vehicles from 1976 onward. An out of state smog test is not accepted. Some newer vehicles qualify for an exemption or a smog abatement fee, so check the [DMV smog page](https://www.dmv.ca.gov/portal/vehicle-registration/smog-inspections/) for your model year before you book.

Utilities are not one company county wide. Anaheim Public Utilities is the only city owned electric utility in Orange County, per the [City of Anaheim](https://www.anaheim.gov/2104/About-Electric-Services). Most of the rest of the county gets investor owned electricity and a patchwork of water districts. Find out which ones serve your address before you sign.

Voter registration runs through the [Orange County Registrar of Voters](https://ocvote.gov/registration/voter-registration-info). Pet licensing runs through [OC Animal Care](https://animalcare.oc.gov/licensing/how-obtain-pet-license) for many cities, though some run their own. Set mail forwarding for 12 months, because tax documents follow the old address for a while.

## Taxes when you cross state lines

The year you move is a split year for California tax purposes, and the split is decided by your residency date, not the calendar.

Become a resident in September and you file a part year return. California taxes all income you earned while a resident, plus California source income you earned while you were not. Income from your old state, earned before the move, is generally outside California's reach. The [Franchise Tax Board's part year resident page](https://www.ftb.ca.gov/file/personal/residency-status/part-year-and-nonresident.html) sets out the framework. Your old state will want its own part year return for the same year.

The larger trap is the old house.

Sell it before you become a California resident and the gain usually stays outside California's reach. Sell it after, and part of that gain can follow you onto a California return. The order of operations matters, so have the CPA conversation before the house hunt, not after you close.

Keep records that support your date. Moving receipts, the lease or closing statement on the new house, utility start dates, DMV paperwork, payroll records showing when the California job began. If the residency date is questioned, those documents are the answer.

## Renting first, or buying right away

I will give you the honest version, because it is more useful than a pitch.

Buying right away works when you already know the submarket. You have visited several times, you know where you will work and how you will get there, and neither the school nor the commute is going to change next year. Renting would cost that household a move and a year of rent. The [rent versus buy math](/blog/renting-vs-buying-orange-county/) shows how long the round trip takes to recover, and I will not redo it here.

Renting first is the better call when you have been here twice, when the office is somewhere in Irvine and the team is still deciding, when your spouse will look for work after the move, or when you are choosing between north and south county and genuinely do not know yet.

Twelve months of rent buys information a weekend cannot. You learn the commute at 7:40am on a Tuesday. You learn that the street you loved online backs onto the 5 and is loud at 11pm.

The cost is real: two moves, a year of rent, and the chance prices move while you wait. It usually pays for households that are guessing, and it usually does not for households that already know.

## What to do in your first week in Orange County

Five actions, done before the routine takes over.

**Book the DMV appointment.** The 20 day and 10 day clocks are already running, and the busier offices go first. Do this before you unpack.

**Drive your real commute at your real start time.** Not on a Saturday. Not at noon. Set an alarm and drive it on a weekday morning, then home at 5:30.

**Walk a street you like at 6pm, then again at 10pm.** Parking, noise, dogs, and how many people are actually outside. A neighborhood that feels quiet at 2pm can be a different place after dark.

**Get an insurance quote on one specific address.** Not a range, not a city. One address, with the roof age and construction type.

**Call the school district with the exact address.** Ask for the assigned schools in writing and ask whether the boundary has changed recently. If you are renting, ask what documents they need to enroll.

The rest of the list can wait a week. Those five cannot.
