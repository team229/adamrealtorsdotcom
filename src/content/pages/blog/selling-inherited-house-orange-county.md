Most families call me about the price. The price is not the first thing that matters, and that is usually a relief.

What matters first is a line on the deed: how your parent held title. That line decides who is allowed to sign a listing agreement, whether a judge gets involved, and how long the whole thing takes. Price comes after that, and it gets easier once you know who signs.

Three arrangements cover most of the inherited homes I see in [Anaheim](/anaheim-ca-home-for-sale/), Fullerton, and Garden Grove.

## Start with the deed, not the price

It takes an afternoon, and it answers most of the questions below.

Three places to look. The Orange County Clerk-Recorder has the recorded deed. The title company that insured your parents' last purchase keeps a copy. The attorney who wrote the will or trust has one too.

When you have it, read the words after your parent's name:

- **"as trustee of the Shaaban Family Trust dated..."** The house is in a trust.
- **"as joint tenants"** or **"as community property with right of survivorship"** Title passed to the survivor automatically.
- **"a married man as his sole and separate property"**, or just the name alone. This is the probate path, most of the time.

Here is the one that catches families. The trust exists, the binder is in a drawer, and the house was never transferred into it. That happens with a home bought after the trust was signed, or refinanced with a lender who wanted it out of the trust. If the house is not titled in the trust, the trust does not help with the house. You are in probate.

## The three paths

The deed decides which row you are in, not the will.

| How title was held | Who can sign | What it takes | Where it usually stalls |
|---|---|---|---|
| Revocable living trust, with the house titled in the trust | The successor trustee, signing as trustee | Death certificate, certification of trust, trust administration | Trustee duties, notices to heirs, siblings who expected a different plan |
| Joint tenancy, or community property with right of survivorship | The surviving owner, alone | Death certificate recorded, title company review | The survivor owns it, but other heirs may expect a share of the value |
| Parent's name alone, no trust | The court-appointed personal representative, after letters are issued | A probate case, then letters, then a sale | Time, statutory fees, the court calendar |

There is a fourth case. The house is in several names as tenants in common, or a parent added a child to the deed years ago. Then every owner signs, or nobody sells.

## Probate, without the fear

Probate is a court process for giving one person the legal power to act for someone who has died. That is the whole idea. It is slow and it is public, and it is not the disaster the internet makes it sound like.

If there is a will, it usually names an executor. If there is not, the court appoints an administrator, usually a close relative. Either way, the court issues a short document called letters. Letters testamentary if there is a will, letters of administration if there is not. Title companies want to see those letters. So does a buyer's lender.

In a probate estate, nobody can sell that house until the letters exist. Not you, not your brother, not an agent with a good sign in the yard. A contract signed before that authority exists is not one the estate has to honor, and it usually becomes a personal problem for whoever signed it.

### How long this takes

In California, probate commonly runs somewhere between nine months and two years, and the house can be sold in the middle of it. The stages are a petition and hearing, an inventory and a creditor period, the sale, then a final accounting. You do not wait for the case to close before the sign goes up.

Creditors get four months from the date the court first issues letters, or 60 days after the estate mails a formal notice, whichever is later (Probate Code §9100). That clock runs while the house is on the market, not after.

### The small estate shortcut

The small estate affidavit is the shortcut people ask about, and the number depends on the date of death, not on the year you are reading this. For deaths on or after April 1, 2025, the limit is $208,850 in gross value, and it adjusts again on April 1, 2028. The Judicial Council publishes the current figures on [form DE-300](https://courts.ca.gov/sites/default/files/courts/default/2024-11/de300.pdf).

Two limits matter here. The affidavit transfers personal property, not the house, and the value of the house generally counts toward the $208,850 total. And nothing moves until 40 days after the death.

There is a separate route for a home. If the house was your parent's principal residence and worth $750,000 or less at the date of death, an heir can petition the court for an order confirming succession to it, without a full probate. Above that value, or for a rental or a second home, the answer is usually a regular probate case. Either way, that call belongs to your attorney.

### What the court actually approves

A probate sale does not automatically mean a courtroom auction. Under the Independent Administration of Estates Act, a personal representative with full authority can list and sell real property without a confirmation hearing. The trade is a Notice of Proposed Action, served on the heirs and anyone who asked for special notice at least 15 days before the sale (Probate Code §10586). If nobody objects, escrow closes on normal timing.

Not every estate gets there. The will can forbid independent administration, and the court can grant limited authority instead of full. If an heir objects in writing before the date in the notice, the sale needs court confirmation, where the judge can take overbids in the room.

Ask your attorney which authority the letters actually grant before you list. It changes how offers are written and how long escrow runs.

### The statutory fee, worked through

California sets probate compensation by formula in Probate Code §10810, and the [Judicial Council's calculation form](https://courts.ca.gov/cms/rules/index/seven/rule7_705) restates the same schedule: 4% of the first $100,000 of the estate's gross value, 3% of the next $100,000, 2% of the next $800,000, then 1% on the next $9,000,000.

On a $1,000,000 estate, that is $4,000 plus $3,000 plus $16,000, or $23,000. The personal representative is entitled to the same amount under §10800, so the estate can pay about $46,000 for ordinary services.

Gross value is what counts, so a $700,000 mortgage on that house does not lower the fee. A family member serving as executor can waive their half, and many do. Extraordinary work, such as litigation or a difficult sale, is petitioned for separately.

The selling costs sit on top of that. Escrow, title insurance, and the county transfer tax are in the [seller cost breakdown](/blog/cost-to-sell-a-house-orange-county/).

Now the part that costs more. A house in probate still carries property tax, insurance, utilities, and a mortgage if there is one. The fee is a known number. The months are not.

## If the house is in a trust

The successor trustee takes over. Usually a child, sometimes a sibling.

Three documents do most of the work: the death certificate, the trust, and a certification of trust under Probate Code §18100.5. The certification is a short signed summary that lets a title company confirm authority without reading the whole trust. Escrow will ask for all three.

The trustee also has duties that have nothing to do with the house. Notice goes to heirs and beneficiaries, generally within 60 days under Probate Code §16061.7, and that notice opens a 120-day window to contest the trust. Trust money stays separate from personal money. Beneficiaries are treated evenly, including the one who never calls.

A trust is faster than probate because there is no court calendar. It is not instant. The notice period runs, the accountant needs a year of records, and beneficiaries get to ask what happened to the furniture. A trustee who ignores those requests can end up personally liable.

A trust also does not end a family argument. If the document is ambiguous, or the trustee and a beneficiary disagree, you can still end up in front of a judge.

## What you can and cannot do before authority exists

This is where the expensive mistakes happen.

**Usually fine, and often necessary:**

- Change the locks, secure the garage, put lights on a timer.
- Forward the mail, keep the utilities on. A house with no power cannot be shown and grows mold.
- Keep the insurance in force, and tell the carrier the house is empty.
- Mow, clean, haul, and make emergency repairs like a burst pipe.
- Keep paying the mortgage, taxes, and HOA dues, and keep the receipts.
- Order inspections, get an appraisal, interview agents, ask for a price opinion.

**Not fine, until authority exists:**

- Sign a listing agreement, a deed, or a purchase contract.
- Promise a buyer a closing date, or take a deposit into your personal account.
- Hand out items that were specifically left to someone in the will.
- Move in and stop paying rent elsewhere without telling the other heirs.

Once you have authority, sign in that capacity and name the estate or trust in the contract. "Maria Lopez, administrator of the Estate of Ramon Lopez" is the signature a title company wants. A contract signed as an individual can make you personally responsible for the estate's obligations.

## The basis question

Under [IRC §1014](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/26/1014), inherited property generally gets a new basis equal to its value on the date of death. Not what your parents paid in 1979.

That changes the math more than any repair or staging decision. If the house is valued at $940,000 on the date of death and sells for $950,000 three months later, the taxable gain is roughly the difference, before selling costs. In a lot of inherited sales, that is close to nothing.

How the title was held matters here too. Community property is treated generously: when the first spouse dies, both halves take the step-up, not just the deceased spouse's half. And if a parent added you to the deed years before they died, that half was a gift, so it may still carry their old basis. Call a CPA before anyone signs a deed.

The record to keep is a defensible date-of-death valuation. Best case, an appraisal ordered for the estate. Also workable: the probate inventory appraisal, or an appraisal done close to the date with the assessor's value and comparable sales attached. Keep it with the deed. Your CPA will want it.

The property tax side is separate from the income tax side. Whether the house keeps your parent's assessed value depends on Proposition 19 and how the transfer is reported, which I covered in the [property tax guide](/blog/orange-county-property-taxes-explained/). Do not assume the bill stays the same.

## The practical work

### Contents

Every family starts with three piles: keep, give away, throw away. The keep pile is the problem. It should go to a storage unit you are paying for, not the garage you are trying to empty. Photograph each room before anything leaves, and check the will for specific gifts. A coffee table promised to a cousin can do more damage than the money.

### Insurance and the empty house

Tell the carrier the house is vacant. Vacancy is usually excluded or limited on a standard policy, and a dwelling fire policy is the normal answer. Ask what it covers for water damage and vandalism, the two claims an empty house actually produces.

### Deferred maintenance

A 1960s ranch here usually has original plumbing, an electrical panel whose label may not match what is inside, single pane windows, and a roof with a story. Two honest options. Sell as-is, disclose what you know, and let the buyer price it. Or do the two or three items that change how the house shows. I would not remodel a house I am about to leave.

### The sibling conversation

Have it before the listing appointment, not during escrow. Four questions:

- Who makes the decision, and who needs to be consulted?
- What does each person need from the proceeds, and by when?
- What happens if the house has not sold in 60 days?
- Who pays for the gardener and the utilities until then?

Write the answers down and send them to everyone. If one sibling wants to keep the house and the rest want to sell, the options are a buyout at an appraised value, renting it together, or a partition action where a judge decides. Disagreement is normal. It does not have to be solved in a courtroom.

## How the sale of an inherited house actually works

Once someone can sign, an inherited sale runs like any other Orange County sale. The [seller page](/sellers/) lists what I need from an estate before we list. Then it is offers, escrow, inspections, appraisal, loan, recording. The [escrow walkthrough](/blog/california-escrow-process-orange-county/) covers that sequence in order.

Two things are different. The seller is the estate or the trust, so proceeds go into that account, not your checking account. And the title company wants the letters or the certification of trust early, before it will insure the deed.

Disclosures do not disappear because the seller is a fiduciary. You disclose what you know, and buyers read inspection reports closely.

## What I would do first

Five calls, in this order:

1. **The title company or the recorder**, for the deed.
2. **A California estate attorney**, to confirm the path and whether probate is actually required.
3. **The insurance carrier**, to confirm the house is covered while it sits empty.
4. **A CPA**, before you spend money on repairs or sign anything.
5. **An agent**, once you know who signs. Not before.

That order saves most families a month and one expensive mistake. It also keeps you from listing a house nobody can currently sell.
