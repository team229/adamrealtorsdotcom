Almost every rent versus buy conversation I have starts the same way. Someone puts two numbers on a napkin, the rent on one side and a mortgage payment on the other, and decides from there.

Those numbers are not comparable. One is the total price of housing. The other is a partial price of owning, with several of the largest costs left off the napkin.

The question that does have an answer is narrower. Over the years you actually stay, which path takes less from you, and how much risk did you accept to get there?

## The trap in the monthly comparison

On paper, a $4,551 mortgage payment looks close to $3,900 in rent. That comparison flatters buying, and it does it twice.

First, part of a mortgage payment is principal. Principal is not a cost. It is cash moving from your checking account into an asset you own, and it comes back to you, less the cost of selling, when you sell. Counting it as an expense is like counting a transfer to savings as a bill.

Second, the mortgage payment is not the whole bill. Property tax, insurance, maintenance, HOA dues, special taxes and the eventual cost of selling all sit outside it. In the example below, the full monthly cost of owning in year one is $6,326 against $3,900 in rent. The gap is $2,426 a month, not $651.

That gap does not hold still. Rent rises. A fixed mortgage payment does not. Property tax rises too, but under Prop 13 the assessed value can go up by no more than 2% a year while you own the home. By year ten in the same example, owning costs $6,826 a month and the comparable rent is $5,551. The gap has narrowed to about $1,275.

So the monthly comparison answers a question about year one when the decision is about year ten.

Compare the total instead. Every dollar each path takes over your holding period, minus what you get back at the end. That is a break-even calculation, and one input matters more than the rest. How long you stay.

## What owning actually costs

| Line | What it is | How it behaves |
|---|---|---|
| Mortgage interest | Price of borrowing | Real cost, front loaded. Most interest on a 30 year loan is paid in the first half. |
| Principal | Loan balance you pay down | Not a cost. It is equity, recovered at sale. |
| Property tax | 1% base plus bonds and charges | Rises with assessed value, capped at 2% a year. |
| Homeowners insurance | Covers the structure | Rising, and harder to place in some areas. |
| Maintenance | Roof, HVAC, paint, landscaping | Rule of thumb, 1% of price a year. Uneven. |
| HOA dues | Shared maintenance in an association | Set by the board, can rise, assessments happen. |
| Mello-Roos | Special tax in a facilities district | On the tax bill, not based on value, does not expire. |

Two lines get confused constantly. Interest is the price of borrowed money, and it is gone. In the example below, 85% of the year one payment is interest, year fifteen is still about 64%, and by year twenty the interest share falls below half. Principal is the balance you pay down, and it is your equity. It only becomes the larger share late in the loan.

The cost that never appears on a statement is the opportunity cost of your down payment, meaning the return you gave up by putting that money into a house instead of an investment account. Most rent versus buy articles leave it out.

The second invisible line is at the end. Selling costs money, and the [seller cost breakdown](/blog/cost-to-sell-a-house-orange-county/) has the detail. If you might sell within five years, that cost is not a footnote. It is often the deciding number.

Property tax needs a warning. The bill is the 1% general levy plus bonds and parcel charges, so the effective rate usually runs above 1%. Assessed value resets to your purchase price, then grows no more than 2% a year, according to the [Board of Equalization](https://www.boe.ca.gov/proptaxes/decline-in-value). The mechanics are in [the property tax guide](/blog/orange-county-property-taxes-explained/). Read the actual bill.

## What renting actually costs

Rent is the total price of housing. That is its virtue, and the line that moves.

The list is short. Rent, renter's insurance, a deposit that comes back if you leave the place in good shape, and the cost of moving.

Rent escalation is the part renters underrate. California's Tenant Protection Act caps increases at 5% plus the change in the cost of living, with a 10% ceiling, for most rental housing older than 15 years. For increases taking effect between August 2026 and July 2027, the cap for Orange County works out to 8.7%, according to the [California Attorney General](https://oag.ca.gov/rentcaps). Two caveats. A single family home rented by an individual owner often sits outside the cap, and a vacant unit can be re-priced for the next tenant. The cap protects you where you are, not when you move.

Moving is the other hidden line. A renter who moves every three years pays for it several times a decade, and each move resets the rent to the market.

## The break-even calculation, worked

The method is six lines. It runs in a spreadsheet in an hour.

1. Pick the holding period you expect, not the one you hope for.
2. Add every dollar the owner pays: down payment, closing, mortgage payments, tax, insurance, maintenance, HOA dues, special taxes.
3. Subtract what the owner walks away with: sale price, minus selling costs, minus the loan balance.
4. Add every dollar the renter pays: rent and renter's insurance.
5. Subtract what the renter's money earned instead: the down payment and closing costs, plus the yearly difference in cash cost, invested at an assumed return.
6. Compare. The year the lines cross is your break-even.

Every figure below is an assumption I chose to make the arithmetic visible. None of it is a quote or a forecast.

| Assumption | Value |
|---|---|
| Price, 20% down | $900,000, $180,000 down |
| Loan | $720,000 at 6.5%, 30 year fixed, payment $4,550.89 |
| Closing costs | 2.5% of price ($22,500), an assumption |
| Property tax | 1.1% in year one, assessed value up 2% a year |
| Insurance | $2,400 in year one, up 5% a year |
| Maintenance | 1% of price in year one, up 3% a year |
| HOA and Mello-Roos | None here |
| Comparable rent | $3,900 a month, up 4% a year |
| Renter's insurance | $240 a year, held flat |
| Home value growth | 4% a year |
| Selling costs | 6% of the sale price, a round assumption |
| Investment return | 5% a year |
| Hold | 10 years |

The 2.5% and the 6% are the two numbers to argue with. Neither is a rate card. Both are negotiated line by line, and 6% lands near the middle of what an Orange County sale adds up to once commission, escrow, title, transfer tax and preparation are counted. If your sale costs less than that, the buying case improves by the difference.

The renter side needs one line of explanation, because it is where most versions of this comparison cheat. The renter starts with $202,500 invested, the down payment plus closing costs, then adds the yearly gap between the cash cost of owning and the rent. That gap totals $225,985 over ten years. Invested at 5% with each year's amount added at year end, the account reaches $621,794, so the gain is $193,309, not the balance.

Owning comes out $22,505 lower over ten years, a small margin against roughly $350,000 of cost. The answer is close because I picked assumptions that make it close.

Break-even falls between year eight and year nine. Renting is still ahead by $11,936 at eight years and buying is ahead by $4,095 at nine. Now change one assumption at a time, keeping the ten year hold.

| Change | Result over 10 years |
|---|---|
| Growth of 2% instead of 4% | Renting ahead by $198,513 |
| Growth of 5% instead of 4% | Buying ahead by $148,263 |
| Rent growth of 3% instead of 4% | Renting ahead by $6,330 |
| Return of 6% instead of 5% | Renting ahead by $25,830 |
| Renter invests the down payment but spends the yearly gap | Buying ahead by $88,462 |

Two percent growth versus four percent swings the answer by more than $220,000. That is not a flaw in the arithmetic. It is the finding. A break-even year is not a property of Orange County. It is a property of the assumptions you fed in, and the two that matter most are home value growth and how long you stay.

So run three versions: slow, middle, fast. If one path wins in all three, you have an answer. If the result flips in the middle, the decision is closer than the spreadsheet implies.

One assumption deserves a flag. The renter side credits you with investing the money you did not spend. If you would spend it, the model is wrong, and the last row of that table shows what happens.

## When renting wins

**Your hold is short.** You pay closing costs at purchase and selling costs at exit. A short stay leaves too little time for principal paydown or appreciation to cover them.

**Your income is uneven.** Commission, contract work, a business in its second year. A fixed mortgage is unforgiving in a slow quarter. A lease is easier to walk away from.

**Your reserves are thin.** If closing empties the account, the first failed water heater goes on a card at 25%. That is how a good purchase becomes a bad year.

**Your life is mobile.** A job in another county, a partner in another city, a plan that is not settled. Selling takes months and costs money. Ending a lease takes weeks.

**The price-to-rent ratio is stretched.** Divide the price by a year of rent. In the example above that is $900,000 divided by $46,800, or about 19, high enough that the rent side stays competitive before you count transaction costs. The old shorthand says a ratio under 15 favors owning and over 20 favors renting. It is a rule of thumb with no authority behind it, and it ignores mortgage rates, tax, maintenance and how long you stay. Use it as a first filter, then run the ten year model.

## When buying wins

**You are staying ten years or more.** Nearly every cost of buying is front loaded or one time. Time spreads those costs out.

**Your income is stable.** Not the payment you can make in a good month. The one you can make in the month a car dies.

**You want the Prop 13 lock.** Your assessed value rises no more than 2% a year while you own. If market values rise 4%, the gap compounds quietly. On a $900,000 base, twenty years at 2% puts the assessed value near $1.34 million while a 4% market puts the house near $1.97 million. The owner pays tax on the lower number. A new buyer pays on the higher one.

**You want control.** Renovations, a dog, a workshop, a garden, paint that is not landlord beige. None of it shows up in the table, and all of it is real.

**You do not want a landlord's decisions in your life.** A sale, a family member moving in, a renewal letter with a number you did not choose.

## The non-financial factors that actually decide it

Once the arithmetic is done, the two paths are usually within a few tens of thousands over a decade. At that point the decision is not financial, and pretending otherwise is how people end up in the wrong house.

Security of tenure is the biggest. A lease is a right to stay until it ends. A mortgage is a right to stay as long as you pay. For a household with children in a school, that difference is worth more than the model can show.

Control of the property comes second. Paint, floors, a wall, a kitchen, a pet. Renters negotiate for all of it or go without.

Then there is the part that never fits in a spreadsheet. I have moved clients out of rentals on sixty days' notice because the owner decided to sell. The numbers were fine. The disruption was not.

My read: if the numbers are close, choose the life you want and stop optimizing. The break-even table is a check on a bad decision, not a substitute for one.

## How to run your own numbers on an Orange County house

1. **Use the actual house and the actual rent.** Same bedrooms, same area, similar condition. Start from a listing such as [Anaheim](/anaheim-ca-home-for-sale/), then find what an equivalent home rents for nearby. A county average tells you nothing about either number.
2. **Replace my assumptions with documents.** The seller's current tax bill, the HOA budget and reserve study, an insurance quote for that address. Assumptions are placeholders until a document arrives.
3. **Write down four numbers and date them.** Home value growth, rent growth, investment return, selling cost. Writing them down stops you changing them later to get the answer you wanted.
4. **Test three holding periods.** Your plan, two years shorter, five years shorter. A shorter hold is the stress test, because most of the risk in buying is the risk of selling early.
5. **Check the cash side separately.** If the monthly works but the cash does not, the problem is the down payment, not the house. The [buyer's guide](/buyers/) walks through the search and offer.
