The lines on a seller's net sheet fall into two groups, and mixing them up is where budgets go wrong. Commission is negotiable and moves the total the most. Escrow, title insurance, recording, and the county tax are quoted per transaction and mostly fixed, though you can sometimes shop them. Preparation and credits are yours to decide, so they belong on the sheet as a decision rather than a surprise.

Run the same lines with a different commission and the total moves. At 4% combined on a $1,000,000 sale, the total lands near 6%. At 6% combined it lands near 8%. That is why the percentages quoted around Orange County spread so widely, and why the percentage is the wrong thing to anchor on.

## What does it cost to sell a house in Orange County?

| Line item | What it covers | How it is set | Example on $1,000,000 |
|---|---|---|---|
| Agent compensation | Your listing side, plus any buyer-side contribution you agree to | Your written agreements. No legal rate | $50,000 at 5% combined, an assumption |
| Escrow fee, seller share | Neutral party that holds the deposit, orders title work, and disburses funds | Quoted per transaction, often split with the buyer | $1,500 |
| Owner's title policy | Title insurance for the buyer as the new owner | Quoted against the price | $3,000 |
| County documentary transfer tax | Orange County's tax on the transfer | $0.55 per $500 of net consideration | $1,100 |
| Recording and courier | Filing the deed with the county | Small and fixed | $150 |
| HOA transfer and document fees | Only if the property is in an association | Set by the association | $400 |
| Pre-listing preparation | Cleaning, repairs, landscaping, staging | Discretionary | $6,000 |
| Credits and concessions | Closing-cost or repair credits you agree to | Negotiated in the offer | $5,000 |

Every figure in the last column is an assumption for illustration, not a quote. Your escrow officer's written estimate replaces it line by line. If you are still deciding how to price and prepare, the [seller's guide](/sellers/) covers that separately.

## What do you actually pay your agent in 2026?

On August 17, 2024, the practice changes from the NAR settlement took effect. Three things are true for sellers now:

- Offers of compensation to a buyer's agent cannot be published in the MLS. Orange County listings generally go into CRMLS.
- Buyer-agent compensation is agreed between the buyer and their own agent in a written agreement, separately from your listing agreement.
- A buyer has to sign that written agreement before an agent takes them to tour a home.

One thing did not change. You can still offer buyer concessions, including closing-cost credits, and those can appear in the MLS.

So you negotiate twice. Your listing agreement sets what you pay your own agent. When offers arrive, you decide separately whether to contribute toward the buyer's side. If you say yes, it comes out of your proceeds, which is why it belongs on the net sheet before you sign anything.

Nobody can tell you the right percentage. Commissions are negotiable and not set by law. A quote described as the standard Orange County commission is a habit dressed up as a rule. Make the number answerable to a scope instead. Ask what the marketing plan is and who pays for each piece, who runs the pre-listing walkthrough and builds the disclosure package, and what changes if the house is still unsold on day 30. The National Association of Realtors publishes its own [settlement FAQ](https://www.nar.realtor/the-facts/nar-settlement-faqs) if you want the rules from the source.

Before I take a listing, we build the net sheet with the buyer-side contribution as its own line, so that number gets decided during the offer conversation instead of turning up on the closing statement.

## What do escrow, title insurance, and the transfer tax cost?

Escrow is the neutral clearinghouse. It holds the deposit, orders the title search and preliminary title report, coordinates the payoff, prorates taxes, and disburses funds. In Orange County the escrow fee is often split with the buyer, and the owner's title policy is often paid by the seller. Both are customary rather than mandatory, and the purchase agreement decides who pays what.

In an HOA, add a transfer fee, a document package fee, and possibly a demand for unpaid or pending assessments. The association sets those, and they are not negotiable the way commission is.

### The documentary transfer tax, worked out

Orange County charges $0.55 for each $500 of net consideration, or fraction of $500. That works out to $1.10 per $1,000, or 0.11% of the price. The rate comes from the California Revenue and Taxation Code and Orange County Ordinance No. 2183, and the [County Clerk-Recorder publishes the calculation](https://www.ocrecorder.com/sites/ocrecorder/files/import/data/files/88534.pdf).

The arithmetic on a $1,000,000 sale:

1. Divide net consideration by 500. That gives 2,000 units.
2. Multiply by $0.55. That gives $1,100.
3. If the division leaves a fraction, round up to the next $500.

So $875,250 becomes 1,751 units, because the last one is partial, and 1,751 x $0.55 = $963.05.

Two cautions. Net consideration is not always the headline price: when a buyer takes over an existing loan, the recorder's own examples exclude that loan amount from the taxable figure, while new financing does not reduce it. Escrow runs the calculation either way. And some California cities levy their own transfer tax on top of the county's, so ask your escrow officer whether a city tax applies at your address.

Customarily the seller pays the county tax in California. It is still negotiable, like every other line in the contract.

## What does it cost to get an Orange County house ready?

Prep has the widest range and the least transparency, because you decide it. A deep clean and some yard work cost a few hundred dollars. Staging and repairs on a larger home can run into five figures. My bias: spend on cleaning and presentation first, and don't renovate a house you are about to leave.

The list is usually cleaning, decluttering, minor repairs, curb appeal, an optional pre-listing inspection, and staging. That inspection costs money up front and can save a renegotiation later. On an older home, I would rather find the problem on your schedule than the buyer's.

### Older housing stock: Anaheim, Garden Grove, and Westminster

A lot of the stock in these cities dates to the postwar build-out. Ranch plans, detached garages, mature trees, decades of owner improvements. Some were permitted. Some were not.

What turns up in inspections: aging supply lines, older electrical panels, original windows, room additions with unclear permit history, roofs near the end of their life. Buyers read inspection reports closely and price what they find, so getting ahead of two obvious items usually costs less than the credit you will be asked for later. The [Anaheim listing page](/anaheim-ca-home-for-sale/) shows how I present property there. The same pattern shows up across [Garden Grove](/garden-grove-ca-home-for-sale/) and Westminster.

### HOA country: Irvine and Newport Beach

In Irvine and Newport Beach, part of the cost of selling is administrative. Document packages, transfer fees, architectural rules, and pending special assessments all surface during escrow, and the buyer's lender reads them too. Order the package early. An assessment discovered in week three can reopen a price you thought was settled. The [Irvine listings page](/irvine-ca-home-for-sale/) shows how homes in those tracts are presented, and the [Newport Beach page](/newport-beach-ca-home-for-sale/) covers that market.

### Hillside properties in Anaheim Hills

Slope, drainage, decks, retaining walls, and roof geometry get more attention on a hillside. So does insurance. The inspection conversation runs longer up there.

## What are the carrying costs while your house is listed?

Your mortgage does not pause because the house is on the market. Neither do property taxes, insurance, HOA dues, or utilities. Move out early and you may add a vacant-home insurance policy and two households of expenses.

This is where overpricing gets expensive in a way the closing statement never shows. A listing that sits sixty extra days costs you monthly, and it tends to sell for less than the same house priced correctly on day one. Run the arithmetic before you list: one month of carrying cost against the price gap you are hoping for. If you are buying at the same time, the [mortgage page](/mortgage/) covers the questions worth asking a lender.

## What about taxes when you sell?

Closing costs are money that leaves at the table. Taxes depend on your whole picture, and I am not a CPA. This section is general information, not advice.

**IRC Section 121.** If you owned and used the home as your principal residence for two of the last five years, up to $250,000 of gain is excluded for a single filer, and up to $500,000 for a married couple filing jointly. California generally follows the same exclusion with its own adjustments, so the state number can differ from the federal one.

**Proposition 19.** Homeowners who are 55 or older, severely disabled, or disaster victims may transfer their Proposition 13 base-year value to a replacement home anywhere in California, up to three times. The replacement has to be purchased or built within two years of the sale. If it costs more than the old home, the assessed value gets an upward adjustment. For a longtime owner, that can be worth more than every closing cost here combined. The [State Board of Equalization guide](https://www.boe.ca.gov/prop19/) has the rules.

**Step-up basis.** Property inherited at death generally gets a new basis, which can change whether selling makes sense now or later. That is an estate planning conversation, not a listing decision.

**Section 1031 exchanges.** Investors selling a rental can sometimes defer gain by reinvesting into like-kind property, within strict identification and closing windows. It does not apply to your primary residence.

Ask a CPA before you list, not after. The answer can change your timing. If you are buying next, the [buyer's guide](/buyers/) covers how the timelines fit.

## Where do sellers in Orange County lose money?

**Pricing to the number you need.** The market does not care what you need for the next house. If the comparable sales point lower, a higher ask buys market time, not money. Market time has a price.

**Skipping the pre-listing inspection on older stock.** In Anaheim, Garden Grove, and Westminster, the buyer's inspector will find the panel, the plumbing, or the unpermitted addition. Finding it first lets you fix it, disclose it, or price for it.

**Reading the offer as a single number.** Price is one line. Credits, repair requests, contingency length, appraisal exposure, and the buyer's financing strength all move your net. A higher offer with a long contingency can cost more than it pays.

**Forgetting HOA documents until week three.** In Irvine and Newport Beach, a special assessment discovered late can reopen a settled price. Order the package when you list.

**Never asking for a net sheet.** Planning a budget before you know your proceeds is guessing. Ask for the estimate in writing before you sign the listing agreement.

## How do you lower costs without cutting the price?

Negotiate compensation openly. It is a business conversation. Compare what each agent will actually do, and put the scope next to the number.

Shop title and escrow where the contract allows, and get more than one quote for the seller's share and the owner's policy.

Do the cheap prep yourself. Cleaning and yard work cost time, not much money, and they change how a house photographs.

Get the inspection before the buyer does. It turns a renegotiation into a planned expense.

If you need to give something to close, consider a credit rather than a price cut. It solves the buyer's cash problem while keeping the contract price intact, which matters for the next appraisal in your neighborhood.

None of this requires discounting the house. It requires knowing which lines are fixed and which are yours.
