Most buyers I meet arrive with a number already in their head. It came from a calculator, a lender's email, or a friend who bought a few years ago. My first job is not to argue with that number. It is to take it apart and see what it is made of.

A calculator is a useful tool. It is also answering a narrower question than the one you are asking, and the gap between those two questions is where budgets get into trouble.

## The number a calculator gives you versus the number you should use

A mortgage affordability calculator takes your income, your monthly debts, a rate, and a down payment, then solves for the highest price that still fits inside a debt to income limit. That is the same arithmetic an underwriter runs. The output is not wrong.

It is a ceiling.

The lender is asking one question: can this household carry the payment? You are asking a different one: can we carry the payment and still live the life we have? Those are not the same test, and the second one has no formula.

Here is what the approval number cannot see. Childcare that costs more than a car payment. A roof that will need replacing in six years. A job that pays well but depends on a bonus. One income that might pause for a few months. A parent who may move in.

I have watched buyers get pre-approved at a number, tour for two weekends, then admit the payment made them uneasy. We reset the range lower and they bought anyway. Nobody I have worked with has regretted buying below their approval. I cannot say the same about the other direction.

So use the calculator. Then subtract. Pick the payment you would still be comfortable with in a slow month, and treat that as your budget. The pre-approval letter is what you can borrow. It is not what you should.

## PITI, and the two Orange County add-ons most calculators miss

PITI stands for principal, interest, property taxes, and insurance. It is the four part payment most calculators model, and it is a decent starting point.

In Orange County, two more lines show up often enough that they belong in the base case, not in a footnote.

### HOA dues

Condos, townhomes, and many planned communities charge monthly dues for shared maintenance, landscaping, pools, and whatever else the association maintains. The amount is set by the association and can change.

The dues count against your debt to income ratio the same way the mortgage does. A $350 monthly HOA adds $4,200 to your yearly housing cost, and at a 43% ratio it takes roughly $9,800 of additional gross income to carry it. That is a constraint, not a rounding error. A large share of Irvine sits inside an association, so the [Irvine listings](/irvine-ca-home-for-sale/) are almost always read with dues attached.

### Mello-Roos

Some newer communities sit inside a Community Facilities District. The special tax that pays for the roads, schools, and utilities built for that district is commonly called Mello-Roos. It appears as a line on the property tax bill rather than a separate invoice, which is why people miss it.

The amount varies by district and can step up over time. It is not the 1% base tax, and it does not disappear once the neighborhood is finished. Two houses a mile apart can carry very different bills. Ask for the seller's most recent property tax bill and read the special assessment lines.

### Property tax is rarely exactly 1%

Proposition 13 sets the base levy at 1% of assessed value, the assessed value resets to your purchase price when you buy, and after that it can rise by no more than 2% a year. Voter approved bonds and parcel charges stack on top of the 1%, which is why the effective rate on a real bill usually runs higher. I am not going to quote you a county average here, because it moves by city and by district, and an invented rate is worse than no rate. The bill for the actual address is the number that matters, and the line items on that bill deserve their own read once you are in contract.

### Insurance is a placeholder

Homeowners insurance in California has become harder to place in some areas, and premiums vary widely by address, roof, and carrier. The insurance line in a calculator is a placeholder. Get a real quote before you fall for a house, especially on a hillside or an older home.

Every one of these lines raises the payment. And every dollar of payment raises the income a lender needs to see. At a 43% debt to income limit, each $100 of monthly payment requires about $2,790 of gross annual income. Keep that conversion rate in your head when you compare two houses.

## Run it backwards: from payment to price

Most tools start with a price and hand you a payment. Try the reverse. The payment is the number you feel every month, so start there and let the price fall out of it.

1. Decide the total monthly housing payment you want. Include taxes, insurance, HOA dues, and any special tax.
2. Estimate the non loan pieces for the kind of home you are targeting. The tax bill and HOA documents for a specific address beat any average.
3. Subtract those from your target. What is left is principal and interest.
4. Turn that into a loan amount at your rate. At 6.5% on a 30 year fixed, each $1,000 borrowed costs about $6.32 a month. A lender will run your real rate.
5. Add your down payment to the loan amount to get a price.

Here is the forward version first, so you can see the shape of it.

|---|---|---|
| Purchase price (one time) | Assumed | $900,000 |
| Down payment, 20% (one time) | Assumed | $180,000 |
| Loan amount | $720,000 at 6.5%, 30 year fixed | |
| Principal and interest | Rate assumed | $4,551 |
| Property tax at 1.1% effective | Rate assumed | $825 |
| Homeowners insurance, $1,800 a year | Assumed | $150 |
| HOA dues | Assumed | $350 |
| Mello-Roos, $3,000 a year | Assumed | $250 |
| **Housing payment** | | **$6,126** |
| Other monthly debts | Assumed $600 | |
| **Total for debt to income** | | **$6,726** |
| Income needed at 43% | Arithmetic | About $15,640 a month, or $187,700 a year |

Payment lines are monthly. Price and down payment are one time figures. Every number there is an assumption for illustration, not a quote or a market prediction. Change the rate, the HOA, or the special tax and the income requirement moves with them. Remove the $350 HOA and the $250 special tax and the same house needs about $171,000 instead of about $188,000.

Now the other direction. Say $6,000 a month is the payment you are comfortable with, and you are looking at a home with the same tax, insurance, dues, and special tax as the table. Take out $825, $150, $350, and $250, and you have $4,425 for principal and interest. At 6.5%, that supports a loan near $700,000. With 20% down, the price lands around $875,000.

Compare that with the table: $6,126 a month buys about $900,000, and $6,000 buys about $875,000. A payment $126 lower costs you roughly $25,000 of house. Small moves in the monthly number move the price more than most buyers expect. The arithmetic does not care which direction you run it. Your comfort level does.

One more piece if your down payment is under 20%: mortgage insurance usually joins the payment. Some calculators include it, some estimate it low. Ask the lender for the number with and without.

## What lenders actually look at

Underwriting is less mysterious than it looks. Six things carry most of the weight.

**Income stability.** Two years of steady income in the same line of work is the usual expectation. A new job in the same field is often fine. A new job in a new field with commission is a longer conversation.

**Debt to income.** The lender adds every minimum payment on your credit report, adds the new housing payment, then divides by gross monthly income. The 28/36 convention and the 43% Qualified Mortgage benchmark are starting points, not rules. Lenders set their own caps and can go higher or lower with compensating factors.

**Credit.** Your score affects the rate, and if you are under 20% down it also affects mortgage insurance. A quarter point on a $720,000 loan is not a small amount over 30 years. Ask what paying down a card or two would do to your pricing before you apply.

**Reserves.** Many lenders want cash left in the bank after closing. The amount varies by loan type and program, and it is usually expressed in months of the full housing payment. Jumbo loans and investment properties tend to ask for more.

**Down payment source.** Seasoned savings, a documented gift, or an assistance program. Cash that appeared last week needs a paper trail.

**Self employment.** Two years of returns, a profit and loss statement, and a year to date balance. The lender uses net income after deductions, so a strong gross with heavy write offs can qualify for less than the owner expects. If you are self employed, get the returns to a lender early instead of guessing.

## The cash-to-close trap

The down payment gets all the attention. It is not the number you need on closing day.

Closing costs are separate from the down payment. On the [mortgage page](/mortgage/) I use a planning range of 2% to 5% of the price, and the exact figure depends on the lender, the loan program, the title and escrow quotes, and any credits you negotiate.

Then there are the costs no calculator asks about. Moving. A refrigerator, blinds, a locksmith. The first utility bill in a bigger house. The small repairs you find in week one. A house is not move in ready just because it closed.

| Cash line | Assumption |
|---|---|
| Down payment, 20% of $900,000 | $180,000 |
| Closing costs at 2.5% | $22,500 |
| Moving | $3,000 |
| Immediate repairs and setup | $5,000 |
| Reserves the lender wants to see after closing | $20,000 |
| **Cash you need available** | **$230,500** |

Seller credits can offset part of the closing cost line. They generally do not reduce the down payment. And the reserve line has to exist in your account after closing, so it counts even though you do not spend it.

If you already own a home in Orange County, part of that cash is probably tied up in it. Proceeds from your sale can fund the next down payment, but the two transactions have to be sequenced, and the [seller side of the move](/sellers/) carries its own costs and calendar. Ask a lender how the timing has to work before you write an offer on the next house, because the answer changes what you can carry in the gap.

## How much income do you need in Orange County?

Add your monthly housing payment to your other monthly debts, divide by your debt to income limit, then multiply by twelve. That is the whole formula, and it gives the gross annual income a lender needs to see. At a 43% limit, every $1,000 of total monthly payment needs about $27,900 of gross annual income to carry it.

The honest answer is that it depends on the price, the rate, the HOA, the special taxes, and your other debts. Anyone who gives you one number without asking those questions is guessing, even when the number has a decimal point and a source line. The table above runs the formula once on a $900,000 house so you can see where each line comes from.

For context, the California Association of Realtors' [third quarter 2026 affordability index](https://mynewsla.com/orange-county/2026/10/30/housing-affordability-holds-steady-statewide-in-third-quarter-2026/), as reported by MyNewsLA on October 30, 2026, put the countywide median existing single family home at $1.47 million, with a monthly payment of $9,360 and a minimum qualifying income of $374,400. Read the assumptions before you compare it with your own budget. That payment covers principal, interest, taxes and insurance, so it leaves out HOA dues and any Mello-Roos tax on a particular address. It also works out to 30% of the qualifying income, not the 43% used above, which tells you how much of a household's income the index assumes housing can take. It is the middle of the county, not your block, and not a condo in Garden Grove.

For a specific target, run the five steps above with the actual tax bill and the actual HOA documents for the address. That is the only version of this math that means anything. ## Three ways buyers get this wrong

### Stretching to the approval limit

The approval is a maximum, and maximums are set by risk models, not by your life. A payment that leaves nothing for savings, repairs, or a bad month turns a house into a monthly emergency. The lender does not lose sleep over that. You do.

### Forgetting the HOA and the special tax

Two houses at the same price can carry payments that differ by several hundred dollars a month. Read the tax bill and the HOA documents before you write an offer, not after the appraisal. In an association, also ask about pending assessments. A special assessment approved last spring can follow you into the purchase.

### Draining the reserves

Using every dollar for the down payment feels efficient. Then the water heater fails, or the furnace, or the roof, and the credit card becomes the emergency fund at a high rate. Keep a reserve. The lender may require one anyway.
