The paperwork is not the hard part. The calendar is.

Most escrows I handle in Orange County do not fail. They drift. A signature comes back a day late. An appraiser needs a second visit. A lender asks for a bank statement you already sent twice. None of it is dramatic. All of it moves the closing date, and the closing date is the one thing you cannot renegotiate with your landlord.

So here is the order things happen in, and the parts that actually decide the outcome.

**The short version:** Escrow opens when the signed contract and the earnest money reach the escrow holder. Title work, disclosures, inspections, appraisal, and loan approval run on overlapping clocks, then the Closing Disclosure, signing, funding, and recording close it out. A financed Orange County purchase usually takes 30 to 45 days.

## What escrow actually is

Escrow is a container. Two parties who do not fully trust each other put money and documents into it, and a neutral third party, the escrow holder, releases them when the agreed conditions are met.

In Southern California the escrow holder is usually an independent escrow company licensed by the California Department of Financial Protection and Innovation. In Northern California it is more often a title company, which is regulated as an insurer. Either way the job is the same.

The escrow officer opens the file, holds your deposit in trust, orders the title search, drafts the instructions, requests payoff statements, collects HOA demands, prorates taxes, prepares the settlement statement, confirms the money arrived, sends the deed to the Orange County Clerk-Recorder, and disburses.

What they will not do matters just as much. They will not advocate for you. They will not tell you whether a repair request is reasonable. They will not decide who is right when buyer and seller disagree. They cannot act without mutual written instructions. Neutral means neutral. If you want someone whose only loyalty is yours, that is your agent or your attorney.

Escrow does not enforce the purchase agreement either. Fixtures, contingency removal, liquidated damages, mediation. Those live in the contract, so ask your agent or a lawyer.

## The steps, in order

### 1. The earnest money deposit

The buyer sends the deposit to escrow, never to the seller or an agent. The C.A.R. purchase agreement prints a three-business-day deadline after acceptance, and that is negotiable. So is the amount. There's no legal percentage.

### 2. Escrow opens

The signed contract reaches the escrow holder, who opens a file and assigns a number. Usually a day or two after acceptance.

### 3. Escrow instructions

The officer drafts written instructions: price, loan, dates, who pays which fee. Both sides sign. Those instructions are the officer's only authority, so if a line does not match your contract, raise it before signing.

### 4. Title search and preliminary title report

The title company searches public records and issues a preliminary title report: owner of record, loans, liens, easements, recorded restrictions. Read it. Old judgments, unpaid taxes, and missing reconveyances surface here, and early is the only good time for them to surface. The file will end with two policies: a lender's policy your mortgage requires, and an owner's policy that protects you after closing.

### 5. Disclosures

The seller delivers the transfer disclosure statement, natural hazard disclosure, seller property questionnaire, and any HOA package. The form commonly preprints seven days after acceptance for delivery. The buyer's review deadline is 17 days after acceptance. If the seller delivers a required item late, the buyer gets five days after delivery or the original deadline, whichever is later. Those are the printed defaults, and your contract can change them. If there is an HOA, the [condo versus house guide](/blog/condo-vs-house-orange-county/) covers what belongs in that package.

### 6. Inspections

General, sewer, roof, foundation, chimney, pool. Whatever fits the house. You're buying information, not a punch list. Which add-ons are worth paying for in this county is covered in [what a California home inspection actually covers](/blog/home-inspection-california-what-buyers-should-know/).

### 7. Appraisal

The lender orders it. The appraiser inspects and compares the home to recent sales. You're not the client here. The lender is.

### 8. Loan approval

Underwriting reviews income, assets, and the appraisal, then issues conditions. Answer each request the same day. A one-day delay on a bank statement routinely costs three days on the back end.

### 9. Contingency removal

Each contingency comes off separately and in writing. This is where the money risk changes, so it gets its own section below.

### 10. Closing Disclosure

Federal rule requires the lender to deliver the Closing Disclosure at least three business days before the loan is consummated ([12 CFR 1026.19](https://www.consumerfinance.gov/ask-cfpb/when-do-i-get-a-closing-disclosure-en-179/)). Compare it to your Loan Estimate line by line. The [CFPB's own explainer](https://www.consumerfinance.gov/owning-a-home/closing-disclosure/) shows what each box means.

### 11. Final walkthrough

A visit shortly before closing to confirm condition and completed repairs. Not a second inspection.

### 12. Signing and funding

Buyer and seller usually sign on different days, often with a mobile notary. Then the lender wires the loan funds to escrow. Nothing is released until the money is confirmed as collected.

### 13. Recording, then keys

Escrow sends the grant deed to the Orange County Clerk-Recorder. In California, a delivered deed is what transfers title, but recording is what gives the world notice and protects you against a later buyer or lender. It's also the step escrow waits for. Until the deed records, escrow will not treat the sale as closed, even if you have signed everything. Once recording is confirmed, escrow disburses: payoff to the seller's lender, proceeds to the seller, fees to everyone else. Then you get the keys.

## The three contingencies, and why removing them is a decision

A contingency is a contractual right to cancel. Removing it is you deciding that a specific risk now belongs to you.

**Investigation.** The broadest of the three. It lets you cancel if you are unhappy with the condition of the property or anything affecting it. Remove it and you accept the roof, the foundation, the drainage, and the neighbor's fence. On a 1950s Anaheim tract home, this is the one I protect hardest.

**Appraisal.** Narrow. It only covers value. If the appraisal lands below the price, you can renegotiate or cancel. Remove it and the gap is yours in cash.

**Loan.** This covers your ability to qualify for the loan described in the contract. It does not cover your down payment, your closing costs, or a low appraisal once the appraisal contingency is gone. That's the most common misunderstanding in the file.

The C.A.R. form's printed default is 17 days after acceptance for the investigation, appraisal, and loan contingencies. The 2014 to 2021 version gave the loan 21 days, which is why that number still circulates. C.A.R. revises the form, so read the dates on your signed contract rather than trusting any general rule.

Contingencies do not expire on their own. When the period passes, the seller can serve a Notice to Buyer to Perform, which gives the buyer two days to remove the contingency or cancel. If the buyer does neither, the seller may cancel the agreement. Silence is not removal. It is the advantage moving to the other side.

If you remove a contingency and then walk away, your deposit is exposed. For a dwelling of one to four units that the buyer intends to occupy, [California Civil Code 1675](https://leginfo.legislature.ca.gov/faces/codes_displaySection.xhtml?lawCode=CIV&sectionNum=1675) governs a liquidated damages clause that both sides initialed. At 3% of the purchase price or less, the clause is valid unless the buyer shows the amount is unreasonable. Above 3%, it is presumed invalid unless the seller proves the number is reasonable. That is a rule about your contract, not a guarantee about your file.

## The timeline, honestly

| Phase | Typical range | What moves it |
|---|---|---|
| Deposit and file opening | 1 to 3 business days after acceptance | Whether the wire arrives on time |
| Title search and preliminary title report | About 2 to 5 business days | Recorder volume, and how fast liens clear |
| Seller disclosures | 7 days after acceptance by contract default | Whether an HOA package is involved |
| Inspections | Inside the investigation window | Access, scheduling, how many reports you order |
| Appraisal | Commonly 1 to 2 weeks from order to report | Appraiser availability |
| Underwriting to clear to close | Often 2 to 4 weeks on a financed purchase | Conditions, and how fast documents come back |
| Closing Disclosure review | 3 business days minimum, by federal rule | Whether the disclosed terms changed |
| Signing to funding | 1 to 3 business days | Notary scheduling and the lender's review |
| Funding to recording | Same day to next business day | County recording cutoffs |

Add those up on a smooth file and you land near 30 days, which is why 30 days appears in most Orange County contracts. It is a convention, not a statute. A cash purchase can close in two weeks. A probate sale with a title problem can run 90.

The delays I see most: appraisal turnaround, lender conditions that surface in week four, a lien that should have cleared in week two, slow HOA documents, and repair work that slips.

## The wire fraud warning

I am going to be blunt, because wire fraud is the one part of escrow where you can lose a down payment and never see the house.

Criminals read real estate email threads. They compromise one mailbox, watch the conversation, learn the closing date and the amount, then send instructions that look exactly like your escrow officer's. The domain is one character off. The signature block is copied. The account number is theirs. The money leaves your bank in minutes, and recovery depends on someone calling fast enough to freeze the receiving account.

So there is one rule: before you wire anything, call the escrow officer at a number you obtained independently. Not the number in the email. Not the number in the signature block. The number on the escrow instructions you received at opening. Read the account number back and have them confirm it.

Two supporting rules. Escrow companies essentially never change wiring instructions mid-transaction, so a "revised" instruction is an alarm, not an update. And no legitimate officer will ask you to wire money by text.

Sellers, this applies to your proceeds account too. Same call, same rule.

## What buyers should do at each stage

- **Days 1 to 3:** Wire the deposit only after phone verification. Sign the escrow instructions after comparing them to the contract.
- **Days 1 to 7:** Book inspections immediately. Good inspectors are scheduled a week out.
- **Days 3 to 10:** Read the preliminary report and the disclosures. Ask about anything you do not recognize.
- **Days 10 to 17:** Decide on the appraisal and loan contingencies before the date, not on it.
- **Final week:** Compare the Closing Disclosure to the Loan Estimate. Do the walkthrough. Bring photo ID.

## What sellers should do at each stage

- **Before acceptance:** Gather permits, warranties, improvement records, and HOA documents. Request your payoff statement.
- **Days 1 to 7:** Deliver disclosures on time. Late disclosures push the review period and the closing date together.
- **Days 7 to 17:** Respond to repair requests in writing. Decide what you will fix and what you will credit.
- **Final two weeks:** Keep the home insured, utilities on, and accessible for the appraiser and the walkthrough.
- **Closing:** Sign the deed with a notary. Confirm where your proceeds go, and verify that account by phone too.

The [seller's guide](/sellers/) covers pricing and preparation. Before you agree to a credit, work out what it does to your net proceeds, because a credit and a price cut are not the same number once fees are recalculated.

## What can go wrong, and what it costs

**Appraisal gap.** The appraisal comes in under the price. The difference comes from cash, from the seller's price, or the deal ends. On a $900,000 purchase with 10% down, a $25,000 gap is not a rounding error.

**Loan denial.** Underwriting finds a debt nobody disclosed, or income that cannot be documented the way the file needs. If the loan contingency is open, you can cancel. If it is gone, the deposit is in play.

**Title defect.** An old judgment, an heir never on the deed, a signature from 1998 that does not hold up. Title can usually cure these. "Usually" is doing real work in that sentence, and the clock does not stop while they try.

**Undisclosed lien.** A mechanic's lien or an unpaid HOA assessment nobody mentioned. The seller clears it, or it becomes a negotiation, or a reason to walk.

**HOA surprise.** A special assessment approved two months ago, a litigation disclosure, a rental cap that is already full. The documents are supposed to tell you. Read them, and read the minutes.

**Cold feet.** A hesitant buyer in week three costs the seller a month of carrying costs and a re-listing.

None of these are reasons to fear escrow. They are reasons to know which dates carry risk, and to treat a contingency removal as a decision rather than a formality.
