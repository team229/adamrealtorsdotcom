The report arrives as a PDF at night and can run sixty pages. Many of those pages are photographs of things that are fine.

I have watched buyers read the first finding, see the word "defect," and start renegotiating in their head. Then we reach the actual issue and it's a loose handrail.

An inspection has a narrow job. Here's where it stops.

## What the inspector actually looks at

A generalist walks the house for two to four hours. The scope comes from the standard of practice the inspector follows, usually CREIA, ASHI, or InterNACHI, and from the [state definition of a home inspection](https://california.public.law/codes/business_and_professions_code_section_7195) as a noninvasive physical examination.

Noninvasive means no cutting, no opening, no digging.

**Structure and foundation.** Cracks, floor slope, exposed framing in the attic or crawlspace, posts, beams. Not the footing, the soil under it, or anything inside a slab.

**Roof.** Covering, flashing, penetrations, gutters, and the underside of the deck from the attic. Remaining life is an opinion, not a measurement.

**Exterior and grading.** Stucco, siding, trim, walkways, driveways, and whether the ground falls away from the house. Not behind the stucco, not the soil.

**Plumbing.** Visible supply and drain lines, fixture function, pressure, the water heater, shutoffs, leaks. Not inside walls, under the slab, or the sewer lateral. Water heaters must be braced, anchored, or strapped ([Health and Safety Code 19211](https://california.public.law/codes/health_and_safety_code_section_19211)), and the seller certifies that in writing with the transfer documents.

**Electrical.** Service drop, panel, breakers, visible wiring, a sample of outlets and switches, GFCI protection, grounding. Not the wiring in the walls.

**Heating and cooling.** Operation under normal controls, age, venting, combustion air, filters, visible ductwork. A unit that runs today can fail next month.

**Interior.** Doors, windows, ceilings, floors, stairs, railings, and signs of water intrusion. Cosmetic condition is not the inspector's concern.

**Attic and insulation.** Framing, sheathing stains, insulation depth, ventilation, duct runs. Anything under the insulation is invisible.

**Garage.** Door and opener, fire separation from the house, the slab, and the water heater if it sits there.

Nine areas, one afternoon, one set of eyes. It's a short checklist with a long report attached.

## What a California home inspection does not cover

Nearly every report has a limitations page. Most buyers skip it. It's the most useful page in the document.

The standard exclusions are concealed systems, underground plumbing and sewer lines, the interior of chimneys, soil and geology, cosmetic condition, code compliance, and anything the inspector could not safely reach.

The consequence is plain. A clean report is not a clean house. It means a generalist didn't see a material defect in the parts he could reach.

It's not a code inspection either. A house built in 1962 met the code of 1962. What matters is whether it's safe now.

And it's not a price list. The report can say the furnace is at the end of its life. It won't say what a replacement costs. That's what a contractor's bid is for, and you'll need one before you ask the seller for anything.

## The add-ons worth paying for in Orange County

Specialists cost more and look closer. My rule is to pay for the ones that examine what the generalist physically cannot.

| Add-on | What it looks at | My default |
|---|---|---|
| Sewer scope | The lateral from the house to the street, by camera | Order it on pre-1980 stock, mature trees, or slow drains |
| Chimney level 2 | Flue interior by video scan, plus accessible attic and roof | Order it on a masonry chimney you plan to use |
| Roof certification | The roof, by a licensed roofing contractor | Only when the report is ambiguous or your insurer asks |
| Structural or geotechnical | Slope, drainage, retaining walls, foundations | Order it on a hillside lot with a wall or visible movement |
| Pool and spa | Equipment, surface, barriers and safety features | Order it whenever there is a pool |
| WDO (termite) | Wood destroying organisms and conditions that invite them | Required on VA loans statewide, worth reading closely on any loan |

**Sewer scope.** This is the add-on I would not skip in [Anaheim](/anaheim-ca-home-for-sale/), Garden Grove, Westminster, or Fullerton. Clay and cast iron sewer laterals are normal at that age, and mature trees find the joints. A camera sees cracks, roots, and low spots that a visual inspection cannot. Some plumbing companies both scope and repair. A licensed plumbing contractor may do that, but only with a written disclosure before you authorize the inspection, including your right to a second opinion under section 7197.

**WDO report.** A separate inspection by a Branch 3 operator licensed through the Structural Pest Control Board. It reports active problems in Section 1 and conditions that invite them in Section 2. No state law requires one to sell a house. VA loans require one statewide in California, FHA requires one when the appraiser notes evidence, and many conventional lenders ask anyway.

**Pool and spa.** If there is a pool, order it. Under section 7195, an inspection of a home with a pool has to cover the pool, and the report has to name which of the seven drowning prevention safety features are present: an isolation fence, mesh fencing with a self-closing gate, a safety cover, door and window exit alarms, a pool alarm, and the rest. It also has to say plainly when a pool has fewer than two. What the inspector is not required to decide is whether a feature meets the ASTM or ASME standard. That matters for insurance and, with small children, more than anything else in the file.

**What I usually skip.** Mold sampling without a known moisture source tells you spores exist, which they do nearly everywhere. Fix the water first. Radon is not a standard add-on and the maps are coarse. Spend the money on the sewer.

## How to read a report without panicking

Start at the summary. Then sort every finding into three buckets.

**Safety.** Dead smoke alarms, no carbon monoxide alarm, exposed wiring, a gas odor, a blocked flue, a loose stair rail, a pool without a barrier. California requires smoke alarms (Health and Safety Code 13113.7), and it requires carbon monoxide alarms in any home with a fossil fuel burning heater or appliance, a fireplace, or an attached garage ([17926](https://california.public.law/codes/health_and_safety_code_section_17926)). Battery-only smoke alarms sold here must carry a non-replaceable battery rated for ten years (13114). Some cities add point-of-sale rules, so check with yours.

**Active water.** A stain under a sink, a leaking supply line, a roof leak, moisture at a sliding door, grading that pushes water at the foundation. This becomes mold and rot.

**Deferred maintenance.** Caulking, an old water heater, single pane windows, a fence, paint. Price lines, not emergencies. Most of it lives here.

Watch for "further evaluation by a licensed contractor." It means the inspector saw something he could not resolve without opening it. Call the trade.

Expect the language to sound worse than the house. Reports are written defensively.

## What older Orange County stock tends to show

Anaheim, Garden Grove, Westminster, and Fullerton carry a lot of 1950s to 1970s housing. The same list comes up. None of it is a reason to walk. All of it is a reason to look.

**Drain lines.** Cast iron waste lines corrode from the inside. Galvanized supply lines scale shut.

**Panels.** Federal Pacific Stab-Lok and Zinsco are the two names that come up most, and both are brands electricians and insurers flag. A replacement is a known scope of work. Have an electrician look.

**Aluminum branch wiring.** Used for a stretch in the late 1960s and early 1970s. Connections loosen and heat. Ask your insurer before you remove contingencies.

**Original windows.** Single pane aluminum sliders. Not a defect. A utility bill issue.

**Unpermitted additions.** A converted garage, a bonus room. The county record and the house stop matching. Pull the permit history.

**Settling.** Slab cracks are common and mostly old. Whether one is moving is not a question one visit answers.

**Roots.** Mature ficus, eucalyptus, sycamore. The scope finds them. The inspection cannot.

## What hillside and HOA properties add

On a slope, water is the story. Grading, downspouts that empty at the foundation, retaining walls with no visible drainage, decks bolted into a hillside, roof planes that shed onto one spot. An inspector will describe what he sees. He will not tell you whether the wall is engineered correctly or the slope is stable. That is an engineer's call, and on a hillside lot in [Anaheim Hills](/blog/moving-to-anaheim-hills/) it's worth the fee.

Attached homes move the boundary. Roofs, exterior walls, and common plumbing are often the association's. Ask the inspector where the association's obligation starts.

## What to do with what you find

Three responses, and only three.

1. Ask the seller to repair it before closing, with a licensed contractor and a receipt.
2. Ask for a credit and handle it yourself.
3. Price it into your offer and move on.

A gas or electrical safety item is one I would rather see repaired before closing. A deferred item, take the credit. Something you intend to redo anyway, price it and stop arguing.

Get a bid first. The report has no prices, and a credit request without a number is just a mood.

This all happens inside the investigation window. The [escrow walkthrough](/blog/california-escrow-process-orange-county/) covers the sequence. I won't repeat it here.

## Choosing an inspector

California does not license home inspectors. The trade runs on a practice act, Business and Professions Code sections 7195 through 7199, which sets conduct rules but requires no state license, no exam, and no pre-license education. Certification through CREIA, ASHI, or InterNACHI is voluntary, and it's the practical quality signal you have.

Ask:

- Which certification, and can I see the number.
- Send a sample report. Look for photographs, clear captions, and a summary you can read in five minutes.
- What insurance do you carry.
- How long will you be on site.
- When will the report arrive. You need it early in your investigation window, not on the last day.
- Will you walk the roof. Some roofs are not safe to walk, and binoculars are not feet on the surface.
- Will you walk me through the findings at the end.
- Does your company also do repairs. Under [section 7197](https://california.public.law/codes/business_and_professions_code_section_7197), performing or offering repairs for an additional fee on a property inspected in the past 12 months is an unfair business practice, and so is paying referral fees to agents.

The fee is small against the decision. A cheap inspection is not a bargain if it's two hours and forty pages.
