Two buyers can walk into the same open house with the same budget and make opposite decisions. That's normal. A condo and a house solve different problems, and neither one is the smarter purchase in the abstract.

Here is the short version. If you want the lowest cash outlay and someone else handling the roof, a condo is often the cleaner fit. If you want control over the property and the widest pool of future buyers, a house usually wins. The rest of this page is the part that decides it for you.

## Condo vs house at a glance

| | Condo | Single family house |
|---|---|---|
| What you own | Your unit plus an undivided interest in the common areas | The structure and the land under it, in fee simple |
| Monthly cost | Mortgage, taxes, insurance, HOA dues | Mortgage, taxes, insurance, plus a maintenance reserve you fund yourself |
| Maintenance | Common areas handled by the association, interior is yours | Everything is yours, inside and out |
| Insurance | Master policy for common areas as the CC&Rs define them, plus an HO-6 for your interior and liability | A homeowners policy for the structure and your liability |
| Rules | CC&Rs, architectural rules, rental caps, pet and parking rules | HOA rules in some neighborhoods, otherwise city code |
| Financing | The project has to meet the lender's requirements | No project review, though condition and appraisal matter more |
| Resale pool | Smaller, because buyers need a lender willing to lend on that project | Broader, since anyone who can finance a house can compete |

None of those rows is automatically good or bad. They are trade-offs, and which one hurts depends on how you plan to use the property.

## The monthly number is not the list price

Two properties listed at the same price are rarely the same monthly cost. This is where most of the surprises live.

**HOA dues.** Ask for the current amount in writing, then ask for the last five years of increases. Dues are set by the board through the annual budget, so you can't shop them the way you shop a lender. A project that kept dues flat for years is not necessarily well run. It may be deferring work.

**Your lender counts the dues.** HOA dues go into your debt-to-income ratio. A higher dues number can reduce what you qualify for, which means the condo may not actually stretch your budget further than the house down the street.

**Special assessments.** These are separate charges on top of dues, usually for a large expense the regular budget does not cover. Sometimes they arrive as one bill. Sometimes they spread over many months. Either way, they belong in your planning.

**Mello-Roos.** These are special taxes that appear on the property tax bill, not the HOA statement. They can attach to either a condo or a house, and they are more common in newer planned communities. Ask for the tax bill, not a verbal estimate.

**Insurance.** A condo owner generally carries an HO-6 policy for the interior, fixtures, and liability. What the association's master policy covers depends on the CC&Rs and on the policy the board bought. It differs from project to project: common areas yes, the building shell and original interior finishes sometimes, your personal property and your liability almost never. A house owner carries a standard homeowners policy. Get real quotes on the specific address, and have your agent read the CC&Rs before you bind. Coastal and hillside properties can price very differently from inland ones.

**The maintenance reserve.** A house does not send you a dues statement, but it still has a monthly cost. Roof, water heater, heating and air, sewer line, exterior paint, fencing, drainage. Set a monthly number with your inspector and treat it as a bill. If you don't, the roof will collect that money for you, all at once.

Run both properties on four lines: mortgage payment, property tax, insurance, and then either dues or a maintenance reserve. Whichever total is lower is the one you can actually afford to keep.

Most of the buyers I sit down with in Irvine start by comparing two list prices. By the end of the conversation they are comparing two monthly totals. The ranking usually changes.

## What the HOA documents tell you

With a condo, the unit is half the purchase. The association is the other half, and you're buying into its finances whether you read them or not.

**The reserve study.** This is an engineering-based estimate of everything the association owns, when each piece wears out, and what replacing it costs. Read the percent funded figure and the funding plan, not just the cover page. The summary normally travels with the association's annual budget report, so ask for both. A reserve study that shows the association is collecting far less than the projected cost of future repairs is telling you where the money will come from later.

**What a thin reserve fund means.** It usually means the association has been keeping dues low by postponing work. The work still comes due. It shows up as a special assessment, a large dues increase, or a project that lenders stop approving. None of those are emergencies you can plan around after you close.

**The budget.** Read where the money goes, especially the insurance line. Ask whether the master policy deductible has increased. A rising deductible shifts more of each claim onto the association, and eventually onto owners.

**Litigation.** Construction defect suits, injury claims, and disputes with a contractor all matter. Litigation can make a project non-warrantable for some lenders, which affects you now and affects your buyer later.

**Rental caps and occupancy rules.** Many projects limit the percentage of units that can be rented, keep a waiting list, or require owner occupancy. These rules can change. A rental cap that is currently full is a closed door for any future investor buyer.

**The minutes.** Read the last twelve months of board minutes. Roofs, plumbing, paving, insurance, and attorney fees all show up there before they show up on a statement. When I review a condo for a client, I read the minutes before the listing photos. The photos never mention a roof.

**The document package.** In a California common interest development, the seller has to give a prospective buyer a document package before title transfers. The list is set by statute: governing documents, the budget and financials, a statement of current assessments, any pending litigation, and the rental rules ([Civil Code 4525](https://california.public.law/codes/civil_code_section_4525)). Board minutes from the last twelve months are available on request, which is how you get the ones worth reading. Your purchase agreement sets the review period on top of that. Do not waive it to win a bidding war. It's the one contingency I wouldn't trade away.

## Financing differences

A house loan is mostly about the borrower, the house, and the appraisal. A condo loan adds a third party: the project.

**Project approval.** FHA financing on a condo depends on the project. [HUD publishes and maintains the approved list](https://entp.hud.gov/idapp/html/condlook.cfm), and you can search it by name, city, or county. Check the status, not just whether the name comes up, because the list also carries expired, rejected, and withdrawn projects. There is a second path called Single-Unit Approval, a loan-level review of one unit in a project that is not FHA-approved. The lender completes HUD's condominium questionnaire instead of the association going through project approval. The project still has to meet [HUD's condo requirements](https://www.hud.gov/program_offices/housing/sfh/handbook_4000-1), and it isn't automatic. Ask your lender which path applies before you fall in love with a unit.

**Warrantable and non-warrantable projects.** Some projects have characteristics that can restrict conventional financing: active litigation, a high concentration of investor-owned units, commercial space beyond a threshold, or a single entity owning too many units. A non-warrantable project is not automatically a bad buy. It does mean fewer lenders, different pricing, and a smaller pool of future buyers.

**Appraisal.** Condo appraisals lean on sales inside the same project and similar nearby projects. One low sale in your building can move your number. House appraisals draw from a wider area, but condition adjustments are larger, so deferred maintenance can cost you at the appraisal stage.

**Timing.** Condo loans can take longer because of the project review. If your contract has a short loan contingency, tell your lender the project name before you write the offer. The [mortgage page](/mortgage/) is where I'd start on the lender questions.

## Where each makes sense in Orange County

I'm not going to tell you one city is cheaper. The answer changes block by block.

**Irvine.** Master-planned villages, a deep condo and townhome segment, and associations with pools, parks, and shared amenities. The documents are usually thorough, which makes them long. Read them anyway. Some of the newer villages carry Mello-Roos on top of dues, so ask for the tax bill. The [homes for sale in Irvine](/irvine-ca-home-for-sale/) list shows how those tracts sit against each other.

**Newport Beach.** Coastal condos sit close to the water and close to each other, and building condition and insurance matter more there than inland. Single family homes on the bluff and inland trade space and land for distance from the beach. The [Newport Beach listings](/newport-beach-ca-home-for-sale/) show how the two halves price.

**Anaheim, Garden Grove, and Fullerton.** Tract homes from the 1950s and 1960s dominate here, many on flat lots with driveways, detached garages, and mature street trees. Plenty of pockets have no association at all, which means no dues line and nobody else handling the exterior. That's freedom, and it's also the full maintenance bill, including whatever the last owner postponed. On these homes, a pre-offer inspection is worth more than a lower offer. You can see the range in the [Fullerton listings](/fullerton-ca-home-for-sale/).

**Huntington Beach and Long Beach.** This is the mix. Downtown Long Beach has condo towers with associations and rules. Huntington Beach has tracts with HOAs alongside older neighborhoods without them. Two addresses a mile apart can mean two very different ownership experiences. The [Huntington Beach page](/huntington-beach-ca-home-for-sale/) is a reasonable place to start.

## The exit question

Every purchase is a future listing. The question is not whether you will sell. It is who shows up when you do.

A condo narrows the buyer pool in ways that have nothing to do with how nice the unit is. FHA buyers may need Single-Unit Approval or move on. VA buyers need the project on VA's approved list, and a HUD-approved project can usually get there without a second review. Investor buyers may be excluded if the rental cap is full. Some buyers won't consider an HOA at all. Add a pending assessment or active litigation and the pool gets smaller still.

A house casts a wider net. Anyone who can finance a house can compete for it. What narrows that pool is condition, and condition is the part you control.

I ask this before we tour: if you had to sell in a slow month three years from now, who is buying this from you? The answer for a fourth-floor unit and the answer for a corner lot are rarely the same.

So match the property to your hold period. If you expect to move in three to five years, the convenience of a condo may be worth the narrower exit. If you plan to hold for a decade or more, you will probably sell into a market you cannot picture today, so the property with the wider buyer pool is the safer bet.

One more thing. HOA rules can change while you own the unit. A rental cap vote or a large insurance increase can affect your plans without anything changing in your own four walls. That's the risk you accept in exchange for someone else handling the roof.

## A decision checklist

Answer these eight before you write an offer. If you can't answer one, that's the next call to make.

1. **What is the full monthly number?** Mortgage, taxes, insurance, and then either HOA dues or a maintenance reserve.
2. **How have the dues moved?** Ask for five years of increases and the current budget's insurance assumption.
3. **What does the reserve study say?** Look at funding levels and any deferred work the association has already identified.
4. **Is anything pending?** Special assessments, litigation, insurance claims, or a board vote on a large project.
5. **What are the rental rules today?** And can the board or membership change them?
6. **Is the project approved for my loan?** And would it qualify for an FHA buyer when I sell?
7. **Who repairs what?** Interior, windows, roof, plumbing, heating and air. Get the boundary in writing.
8. **If I had to sell in a slow month three years from now, who is my buyer?**
