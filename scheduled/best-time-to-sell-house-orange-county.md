Every January my phone gets a version of the same call. A homeowner in Anaheim Hills or Fullerton has decided to sell, has a date in mind, and wants to know whether to wait for spring.

Sometimes waiting is right. Often it is not, and the reason has nothing to do with the calendar. It has to do with the house, the buyer it attracts, and what the next six months cost while nothing happens.

## Why spring is not automatically better

Spring has the deepest buyer pool. That part is real, and there is a reason for it. Families want to move between school years. Relocation offers land in the summer. Daylight gets longer and people start browsing on a Sunday again.

Here is the part the seasonal advice tends to skip. Every seller knows the same thing. So inventory rises in the same weeks demand rises. The two forces pull against each other, and the net effect on your price is not automatic. It depends on the ratio in your price band and your neighborhood, and that ratio moves every year with rates and with how many owners decided to hold.

There is a second effect, quieter and more useful. In a thin month, a buyer might see four homes that fit. In May they might see twelve. More showings, but also more comparison, more second visits, more reasons to wait for the next listing. Spring hides an optimistic price for a week or two. December exposes it in three days.

I have had a February listing in Anaheim Hills draw a crowd and a similar house in May sit. The difference was the kitchen and the price, not the month.

The closest thing to a national answer comes out every March. [Realtor.com's 2026 study](https://www.realtor.com/research/best-time-to-sell-2026/), published March 18, named April 12 to 18 as the best week to list, and the numbers behind it are smaller than the coverage suggests: homes listed that week historically reached prices about 1.3% above the average week and drew 16.7% more listing views. Read the label before you move your plans. The finding is national and modeled from 2018 to 2025 seasonal patterns, not measured in Orange County, and the report says the ideal week shifts by metro. It is an anchor, not an instruction for your street.

## The two calendars that matter

Forget the seasons for a minute. Two calendars actually decide your date.

**The school calendar.** A family that needs to be in before the term starts has a hard deadline. To close by late July, they need to be in contract by June, which means they tour in April and May. List in March or April and you meet that buyer. List in June and most of them are already in contract, so you're selling to a different pool: downsizers, investors, relocation buyers, people who can close in thirty days. That pool is real. It's just smaller and pickier.

**Your calendar.** When does your lease end. When does the new job start. Is there a divorce filing, a probate case, a trust distribution, a tenant on a lease, a bridge loan clock. Do you need the proceeds for the next purchase, and can you carry two mortgages for a few months if you do not.

The trap is when both calendars belong to the same family. You want to sell before the school year ends because your buyers want that, and you also need to buy before the school year ends, which means you are competing with your own buyers for the same closing window. That's why some sellers in [Irvine](/irvine-ca-home-for-sale/) and Orange deliberately list in fall, rent back briefly, and buy in winter when the competition thins.

Neither calendar is wrong. But you have to name which one is driving, because they point in different directions.

## Month by month, honestly

The table below is a pattern, not a measurement. It is my read of how the county usually behaves and why, drawn from showings and offers rather than from a dataset. Read every row as judgment, not as a finding. It is not a forecast, and it does not override your street.

| Window | Buyer traffic | Competition from other sellers | What it favors |
|---|---|---|---|
| January to mid February | Light, then climbing | Lowest of the year | A seller already prepared and priced sharp |
| March | Rising fast | Rising faster | A house that shows well and is priced ahead of the spring wave |
| April and May | Peak | Peak | Turnkey homes in the most wanted school zones. Less forgiving of unfinished work |
| June | Strong, starting to thin | Still high | Sellers who can close in 30 days for a buyer with an August deadline |
| July and August | Drops, especially late August | Drops with it | Flexible timelines, second-home and coastal property, quiet listings |
| September and October | A second, smaller wave | Moderate | Well-prepared homes, and sellers who missed spring |
| November | Thinning | Thin | A real reason to move and a realistic price |
| December | Thinnest | Thinnest | Short-notice showings, and buyers with a year-end deadline |

Two things follow from that table. The best traffic month and the easiest competition month are not the same month, and the gap between them is where most of the arguing happens. Also, the swings tend to look smaller here than the national averages suggest, partly because the weather rarely shuts a showing down and partly because so much local buying runs on job and family timelines rather than seasons.

Traffic in any given month also depends on what rates did in the six weeks before it. The [Freddie Mac weekly survey](https://www.freddiemac.com/pmms) is the series to watch. A sharp move in either direction brings buyers out or quiets them, and that effect can be larger than the month itself.

Coastal and hillside properties have their own rhythm on top of this. A [Newport Beach](/newport-beach-ca-home-for-sale/) or Huntington Beach home photographed in June gloom looks flat, and the same house in October looks like the listing you wanted. A canyon or Anaheim Hills property in a fire zone can run into insurance scrutiny at any time of year, and that scrutiny is about the policy, not the month. Neither of those is a reason to delay a year. Both are reasons to plan the photography and the insurance conversation before you list. If the property is a summer rental, the rental calendar and the showing calendar also collide in July, and you have to decide which one you are running.

## What actually moves your price

Ranked, with my judgment attached.

1. **Price against the competition, not against last year.** Your buyer is comparing you to what is listed this week. The neighbor's sale from August is context, not a target. This is the largest single lever, and it is the one sellers negotiate with themselves about the longest.
2. **Condition and the first ten seconds.** The smell at the front door, the light in the entry, the state of the driveway. Buyers decide whether they are interested before they reach the kitchen.
3. **Preparation and photography.** Decluttering, paint, lighting, landscaping, professional photos and a floor plan. This is where a modest budget changes the outcome most.
4. **Marketing reach.** MLS and syndication, the agent network, broker previews, private showings, and a follow-up plan for everyone who walked through. Reach does not fix a bad price. It makes a good one travel.
5. **Timing.** Fifth, not first. Timing is a multiplier on a well-prepared house and close to a rounding error on a badly prepared one.

I would rather list a sharp, well-photographed house in December than a tired one in May. That is not a slogan. It is the pattern I see in the offers.

## When waiting costs more than it pays

Waiting is not free. It just bills you monthly.

Take a $900,000 home with a $600,000 loan at 6%. Month-one interest is exactly $3,000, since $600,000 at 6% is $36,000 a year, and it falls by a few dollars a month as the balance drops. Property tax at roughly 1.1% adds about $825. Insurance at $200 a month, and more if the house sits in a fire zone. Maintenance and utilities at $600, which is still under the 1% of value that maintenance alone often runs. That totals about $4,625 a month, call it $4,600. Principal is not in the figure, because it comes back to you as equity. Interest does not. These are illustration numbers, not quotes for your house, but the shape is right, and it is the part sellers forget when they decide to "see what spring brings."

Then the non-financial clocks:

- **Divorce.** The court and both parties have to agree. A delay is not neutral, and it usually costs more in legal fees than in price.
- **Probate.** You cannot speed the court up, but you can control whether the house is ready when authority arrives. If you are on that path, the [inherited house guide](/blog/selling-inherited-house-orange-county/) covers the order of operations.
- **Job move.** A bridge loan, a rent-back, or a vacant house with a security risk. Each month of waiting has a cost attached that is not on the closing statement.
- **Rates.** A drop raises your buyers' budget and also raises the price of your next home, and it usually brings more sellers out to compete with you. Waiting for rates is a two-sided bet.

One more. Transaction costs are set by your price and your contract, not by the month. Escrow, title, transfer tax and compensation look the same in March and in October on the same sale price. Carrying cost is the line that moves with time. That's the honest arithmetic, and it usually argues for sooner than the seller expected. The [seller cost breakdown](/blog/cost-to-sell-a-house-orange-county/) has the line items if you want to run your own numbers.

## When waiting genuinely helps

There are real cases. I don't talk people out of these.

**A renovation three weeks from finished.** Finish it. A half-built kitchen shrinks your buyer pool and your price more than a month of delay costs.

**A tenant on a lease.** A tenant limits showings and narrows your buyers to investors. Either wait for the lease to end or price for the tenancy and market it as an income property. Don't pretend it's vacant.

**A title problem.** An unpermitted addition, a missing reconveyance, an old lien, a name on the deed that should not be there. A buyer's lender will find it during escrow anyway. Fixing it before you list costs weeks. Finding it after you accept an offer can cost the deal.

**An insurance problem.** Hillside and canyon homes in fire zones can face non-renewal or a slow FAIR Plan quote. A buyer's lender needs coverage to fund. Get your own answer first, because a two-week insurance delay in escrow is worse than a two-week delay before listing.

**A probate or trust item that needs a signature.** You can't list what you can't legally sell. Get the authority in place, then list.

## The 90-day runway

If your date is roughly three months out, this is the order I use.

**Weeks 1 and 2: the numbers.** Payoff statement, net sheet, a conversation with a [lender](/mortgage/) if you are buying next, a conversation with a CPA if the gain might be taxable. Decide your real target date, not the ideal one. If you want the escrow mechanics, the [escrow walkthrough](/blog/california-escrow-process-orange-county/) explains what happens after you accept.

**Weeks 3 and 4: the walkthrough.** Room by room, inside and out. Two lists: what signals neglect, and what an inspector will flag. Book the trades now, because painters and cleaners fill up in spring. If the house is older, order a pre-listing inspection so you are not surprised later.

**Weeks 5 to 8: the work.** Paint, floors, lighting, landscaping, deep clean, and the small repairs. In parallel, start the paperwork: the Transfer Disclosure Statement, the Seller Property Questionnaire, the natural hazard disclosure, HOA documents if there is an association. The package takes longer to assemble than sellers expect, and it is easier to build before a buyer is involved.

**Weeks 9 and 10: presentation.** Photography, floor plan, staging if it fits the price band, sign, lockbox, final punch list. Photograph on a clear morning, which in coastal areas may mean waiting for the fog to lift.

**Weeks 11 and 12: live.** Broker preview, first weekend open house, offer review, and a plan for the second weekend if traffic is soft. Soft traffic in the first ten days is information, not bad luck. Use it.

If the house needs nothing, this compresses to about three weeks. If it needs a roof, it does not compress at all. That is the real reason some sellers "wait for spring." They are not waiting for the market. They are waiting for the house.

## The date is a decision, not a discovery

Pick the month your house can be ready, your buyer pool is actually shopping, and your own next step allows. Then put your energy into price, condition, preparation and reach, because that is where the money is.
