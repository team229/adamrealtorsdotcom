You spent the down payment. The wire cleared. You have the keys, and the first thing that arrives is not a housewarming card.

This is the part that never made it onto the closing statement. The first twelve months carry bills that are entirely normal to people who have owned before, and invisible if you have only ever rented. They do not arrive together. That is what makes them hard.

I would rather you meet them on a calendar than on a bank statement.

## The supplemental bill arrives months after closing

Three to six months after closing, the county usually mails a tax bill you have never seen. It is not an error, and it is not the annual bill. It covers the difference between the seller's assessed value and the price you paid, prorated from your transfer date to June 30.

The money is not sitting in escrow. Your impound account collects for the regular tax bill and generally does not collect for this one, and the county does not send a copy to your lender. Proration at closing splits the regular tax with the seller, but it does not touch this bill, because the assessment did not exist until you bought the house.

Close between January and June and you may get two bills: one for the rest of that fiscal year and one for the full year after it. Close later in the year and you will usually see one.

What matters for planning is the shape: a one-time payment, arriving months after you have spent everything, tied roughly to the gap between the old assessed value and your price. The arithmetic, the month-by-month proration factors, and the appeal window are in the [property tax guide](/blog/orange-county-property-taxes-explained/). I won't redo them here.

The calls I get are rarely "what is this." They are "did the county make a mistake." Usually it did not.

Mello-Roos charges ride on the annual bill as their own lines, and the tax guide shows how to check whether a parcel sits in a district before you offer.

## Your first full tax bill is not the seller's

On the day you take title, the county generally resets the assessed value to what you paid. Prop 13 caps the annual inflation adjustment at 2%, but the reset itself has no cap. That single fact explains why your bill looks nothing like the seller's, even on the same street.

The cycle is fixed and it does not care when you moved in. Bills go out in September or October. The first installment is due November 1 and turns delinquent December 10. The second is due February 1 and turns delinquent April 10.

The escrow analysis answers the question I hear most in month thirteen: why did my payment go up. Your servicer collected based on the tax bill it had, often the seller's and often years out of date. If you pay through an impound account, that account is funded with your money, and a shortfall becomes your problem at the next review. When the real bill lands, the servicer recalculates and spreads the difference over twelve months. Federal rules cap that cushion at one-sixth of the year's escrow disbursements, which works out to about two months of escrow payments, and a servicer may hold less. The increase is not a rate change. It is a correction.

One piece of good news: the homeowners' exemption takes $7,000 off the assessed value of your principal residence, and it renews for as long as you own and occupy the home. File it once. The [tax guide](/blog/orange-county-property-taxes-explained/) has the deadline and the partial-year rule.

## Insurance renews, and it can move

Your first year of homeowners insurance is usually paid at closing. That is why month one feels free and month thirteen doesn't.

The renewal lands twelve months after the policy started, which is generally your closing date. That is easy to miss, because by then you are thinking about a bathroom, not a declaration page.

What moves the number: the property's claims history, the age of the roof, reconstruction cost, and the market you are buying in. The market part has been ugly. The California FAIR Plan, the insurer of last resort, won approval for a [statewide average increase of 29.1%](https://www.kqed.org/news/12094860/california-fair-plan-announces-29-1-rate-hike-for-homeowners-this-fall) on its homeowners and dwelling fire policies, effective October 15, 2026. That is an average across the state for one carrier of last resort, not an Orange County number and not your number. If the FAIR Plan is your only option, price the renewal early.

Inland Orange County is not wildfire country the way the canyons are, but hillside parcels around Anaheim Hills price differently from a flat lot in Westminster.

Re-quote sixty days before renewal. Not after. Shopping a policy that has already renewed is harder than shopping one that is about to.

Tell your carrier if the house will sit empty. A renovation that runs long, a property you bought before you sell the old one. Most standard policies restrict coverage once a home is vacant past a set number of days, and a claim denied for occupancy is a bad way to learn that.

## The HOA calendar is not your calendar

Your dues run on the association's fiscal year, not the anniversary of your purchase. That year might start in January or July. The board adopts a budget before it begins, and the budget report reaches you before then. That report is where the increase is announced.

California law puts a ceiling on what a board can do alone. Under [Civil Code 5605](https://leginfo.legislature.ca.gov/faces/codes_displaySection.xhtml?lawCode=CIV&sectionNum=5605), a regular assessment cannot be more than 20% above the prior fiscal year's regular assessment, and special assessments cannot exceed 5% of budgeted gross expenses in the aggregate, without approval from a majority of a quorum of members. That is real protection, and a useful signal. A board that keeps hitting the ceiling is telling you the reserve fund is thin.

A special assessment is the one that hurts. It usually arrives as a separate notice, sometimes with a payment plan attached, and it is not part of the monthly number you budgeted. Ask for the last five years of increases, the current budget, and the reserve study before you close.

Dues are billed monthly or quarterly. If the seller paid through the end of a quarter, escrow prorated it, and your first statement may look short or strange. Read it against the closing statement before you call anyone.

The transfer and document fees you paid at closing do not recur. What might: a move-in fee, a deposit, an architectural review fee for your first project. Small amounts, all at once, in the same month as the movers. The [condo guide](/blog/condo-vs-house-orange-county/) covers what the documents actually commit you to.

## Maintenance is a monthly number even when no one sends a bill

The working rule of thumb is 1% of the purchase price per year, set aside monthly. I want to be plain about this: it is a rule of thumb, not a forecast for your house. A 1972 ranch with original plumbing and a pool is not the same asset as a 2019 townhouse. The rule gives you a starting number so you do not treat maintenance as zero, which is the mistake most first-year owners make.

**Roof.** Twenty to thirty years of life, and an inspector can only report on what is visible. A roof at year twenty-two is a bill in your future, not a maybe.

**Heating and air.** A system that tested fine in April can fail in August, at the worst possible moment, at the highest possible price.

**Plumbing.** Supply lines, the water heater, the sewer lateral. This is the bucket that produces the four-figure emergency rather than the four-hundred-dollar annoyance.

The reserve matters because failure is a timing problem before it is a price problem. A roof does not care that you closed eight months ago and are still paying for window coverings.

## Utilities, tools, and the things you did not own as a renter

The first bill in a house is usually bigger than the last one in an apartment, and it is rarely one thing.

More square footage to condition. A yard that needs water, which in Orange County is a real line item and not a rounding error. A pool pump that runs hours a day. Single-pane aluminum windows, common in the mid-century tracts across Anaheim, Garden Grove, and Fullerton, letting the heat in and the cooled air out.

Some of these bills are new rather than bigger. Water comes from a municipal utility in some cities, like Anaheim, and from a private company in others. Trash is a parcel charge on the tax bill in some cities and a separate account in others. Check what the seller paid and to whom before you budget around a guess.

Then there are the objects. A hose and nozzle. A ladder tall enough to reach the gutters. A mower, or a gardener. Window coverings, frequently the largest single purchase in the first month after furniture. Filters, every one to three months. A water heater flush, once a year, cheap now and not cheap later. A shop vacuum, because something will overflow.

None of those is an emergency. Together they are a month of spending nobody put in the plan.

## The first 90 days

A home inspection is visual and limited. It does not open walls or scope the sewer, and it is not a warranty. The [inspection guide](/blog/home-inspection-california-what-buyers-should-know/) covers what the report can and cannot tell you.

So things break in the first ninety days even after a clean report. In my experience the list is short and repetitive: the water heater, the disposal, a garage door spring, an air conditioning capacitor, irrigation valves, a toilet flange, a dryer vent packed with somebody else's lint.

**A home warranty, if you bought one.** In California this is a [home protection contract](https://www.insurance.ca.gov/01-consumers/105-type/Home-Protection-Contracts.cfm) regulated by the Department of Insurance. Consumer and industry pricing surveys commonly put plans at roughly $350 to $900 a year, plus a trade service fee of $75 to $150 per visit. Those are ranges across companies and coverage levels, not a going rate. Read the exclusions before you need them. Pre-existing conditions and code upgrades are the usual gaps.

**A builder warranty, on new construction.** California's Right to Repair Act, Civil Code 895 and following, sets standards and different time windows for different categories of defect, with an outside limit that is generally ten years from substantial completion. Start with the builder's warranty department, in writing, and keep your dates.

**An insurance claim.** This works for sudden and accidental damage. A supply line that let go and flooded a cabinet is a claim. A water heater that rusted through from the inside is maintenance. Call your agent before you call a contractor.

**Neither. You just pay it.** Most common outcome, and usually the right one. A few hundred dollars in month two is a nuisance. The same problem ignored until month fourteen is a repair plus the damage it caused on the way.

## How to build the first-year buffer

Here is how I build it. One folder of paperwork, twenty minutes.

1. Pull the current annual tax bill for the parcel. Divide by twelve.
2. Take the premium you actually bound, not the first quote. Divide by twelve.
3. Add HOA dues at the current amount.
4. Average three months of utilities. The first month is never representative.
5. Set maintenance at 1% of the price divided by twelve. Raise it if the home is old, has a pool, or the roof is near the end of its life.
6. Estimate the supplemental bill as a one-time line, not a monthly one.
7. Compare the total against what the impound account already collects. The gap is your real number.

A made-up example, to show the shape. Every figure in the table below is an assumption for this example, not a rate quote for Orange County.

| Line | How it is set | Assumption used in this example |
|---|---|---|
| Property tax | Annual bill divided by 12 | $11,000 a year, about $917 a month |
| Insurance | Renewal premium divided by 12 | $2,400 a year, $200 a month |
| HOA dues | Current monthly amount | None in this example |
| Utilities | Three-month average | $350 a month |
| Maintenance | 1% of price divided by 12 | $750 a month on a $900,000 price |
| Supplemental tax | One-time estimate | Not monthly, plan it separately |

That is about $2,217 a month on top of principal and interest, before the one-time supplemental bill that your impound account will not cover.

Then separate the money. Open an account, move the monthly number on the first, and stop treating that balance as available. Review at month twelve, because the impound analysis and the insurance renewal both land around then and both change the figure.

If the gap feels uncomfortable, that is information rather than failure. It usually means the price sat at the top of your range, which the [affordability guide](/blog/how-much-house-can-i-afford-orange-county/) covers.

The first year is a cash flow problem with a known shape.
